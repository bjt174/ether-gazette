📰Headlines

CMS's Proposed 2027 Rule Would Cut Anesthesia's Medicare Conversion Factor for a Second Year Running

On July 14, CMS released its Calendar Year 2027 Medicare Physician Fee Schedule proposed rule, floating anesthesia conversion factors of $20.4165 per unit for clinicians in qualifying Advanced Alternative Payment Models and $20.2143 for everyone else — declines of roughly 0.9% and 1.4% from the rates that took effect this January. Rather than reversing last year's thin increase (our August 10 issue), the proposal would compound it, and it lands as ASA continues pressing CMS over the anesthesia-specific methodology adjustments that have eaten into statutory payment bumps two years straight. Comments on the proposed rule are open through September 14, 2026.

CMS · Calendar Year (CY) 2027 Medicare Physician Fee Schedule Proposed Rule

FDA's Bupivacaine-Contamination Recall Widens to a Third Spinal-Kit Manufacturer

The Class I recall tied to contaminated Huons Co. bupivacaine and lidocaine ampules — the FDA's most serious category — has now drawn corrections or removals from three device makers that packaged the affected drug into neuraxial procedure kits. B. Braun pulled its PENCAN and Spinocan spinal needle kits containing the recalled bupivacaine and, as of an April 24 update, had reported 35 serious injuries tied to compromised injectate, which the FDA warns can carry meningitis and encephalitis risk when introduced into the subarachnoid space. Arrow International corrected its convenience kits in April with no injuries reported, and Becton Dickinson followed on June 3 with a letter instructing facilities to strip the Huons ampules from BD spinal trays before continued use. Programs that haven't already reconciled spinal tray and kit inventory against the recalled lots should treat this as unfinished business, not a closed alert.

FDA · Convenience Kit Recall: B. Braun Removes Spinal Anesthesia Kits Containing Huons Bupivacaine

ResMed's Astral Ventilators Draw a Class I Recall Over a Leaking Supercapacitor

The FDA assigned its most serious recall classification to ResMed's Astral 100 and 150 ventilators on July 28, covering roughly 168,000 devices distributed across 49 states. The failure mode is a supercapacitor that can leak electrolyte over time, damaging the circuit board and pushing the unit into a fail-safe shutdown — potentially mid-therapy, with a high-priority alarm and loss of ventilatory support. ResMed had logged five serious injuries as of late June and no deaths; the company is prioritizing replacement parts for the highest-risk patients first because of limited component supply. The FDA is telling patients and caregivers not to stop using an affected unit without clinician guidance, but anesthesia and critical-care staff who cross paths with Astral-supported patients perioperatively should know the fault signature.

FDA · Ventilator Correction: Resmed Issues Correction for Astral Ventilator

A Widening CRNA Shortage Is Reshaping How Surgery Centers Staff Anesthesia

Workforce projections continue to point toward a strained anesthesia labor market: nearly 30% of practicing anesthesiologists are expected to leave the field by 2033, and the CRNA workforce is projected to fall about 12,500 short of demand by the same year — roughly 22% of today's supply. The pressure is already visible in ambulatory surgery centers, where the share expecting to pay anesthesia stipends jumped from 28% in 2024 to 44% in 2025, and two-thirds of ASC respondents now list anesthesia coverage among their top three financial concerns heading into 2026. For SRNAs entering the market, the near-term picture is one of demand outpacing supply almost everywhere outside the most saturated urban centers.

📚From the Literature

A Meta-Analysis Finds SGLT2 Inhibitors Cut Perioperative Kidney Injury — But the Ketoacidosis Risk Is Real, Not Theoretical

A systematic review and meta-analysis pooling 246,242 surgical patients found that perioperative SGLT2-inhibitor use was associated with a 32% relative-risk reduction in acute kidney injury, with the strongest protective signal in elective surgery, and an approximate 27% reduction in overall mortality — though the mortality finding leaned heavily on one large observational study and needs confirmation. The metabolic side of the ledger was more complicated: primary analyses found no significant increase in diabetic or euglycemic ketoacidosis, but sensitivity analyses that excluded certain studies revealed a real and substantial risk, consistent with the well-described "euglycemic trap" in which ketoacidosis develops despite near-normal glucose, delaying recognition. The authors suggest the FDA's standard 3–4 day preoperative hold may be longer than necessary to capture the renal benefit and that shorter holds — 24 hours or less — paired with vigilant metabolic monitoring may better balance protection against risk; that tension is exactly what this week's Knowledge Pearl unpacks.

Balbaa, E., Gadelmawla, A. F., Ibrahim, A., Manasrah, A., Elbataa, A., Shubietah, A., Elgendy, M. S., Sobhy, A., Mansour, A., Awashra, A., Elguindy, N. N., Bazzazeh, M., & Ben-Selma, A. (2026). Renal protection at a metabolic cost: A systematic review and meta-analysis of perioperative use of sodium-glucose cotransporter 2 inhibitors. Endocrinology, Diabetes & Metabolism, 9(2), e70180. https://doi.org/10.1002/edm2.70180

⚖️Legislation & Scope Tracker

Rhode Island's CRNA Independent-Practice Bill Dies in Committee

H7426, which would have let CRNAs with at least two years of experience practice without physician supervision and directed the state's Medicaid agency to pursue any federal waiver needed for CRNA reimbursement, was introduced January 30 and went dead in committee on June 11 without a floor vote. Had it passed, the change wouldn't have taken effect until January 1, 2027. Rhode Island remains a supervision-required state for now, joining a list of 2026 sessions — Florida's HB 375 among them (our August 10 issue) — where CRNA autonomy bills stalled short of passage despite active sponsorship.

Rhode Island General Assembly · 2026 -- H 7426

A Pennsylvania Lawmaker Presses to Recognize CRNAs as APRNs in Statute

State Rep. Lisa Borowski circulated a co-sponsorship memo for HB 2335, which would amend Pennsylvania's Professional Nursing Law to formally define and expand CRNA scope of practice. The memo leans on now-familiar arguments — CRNAs average more than 9,000 hours of ICU and anesthesia-specific clinical training before certification, and outcomes research shows no statistically significant difference in patient outcomes compared with anesthesiologist-delivered care — to argue that Pennsylvania is out of step with the roughly 40 states that already recognize CRNAs as advanced practice registered nurses in statute. The bill is early in the legislative process, but it keeps Pennsylvania on the list of large, supervision-required states, alongside Texas, New York, and Michigan, where organized pushes for change are active.

💡Knowledge Pearl

SGLT2 inhibitors don't cause the kind of DKA a glucose check will catch. SGLT2 inhibitors — empagliflozin, dapagliflozin, canagliflozin, and others — block glucose reabsorption in the proximal renal tubule, producing glycosuria, mild volume contraction, and a metabolic shift toward fat oxidation and ketogenesis, the same mechanism behind their renal- and cardio-protective effects (see this week's Literature pick). Under the physiologic stress of surgery — fasting, insulin withdrawal, catecholamine surge — that shift can tip into ketoacidosis, but because the drug's glycosuric effect keeps blood glucose from climbing, patients can present with a significant anion-gap ketoacidosis while their glucose reads a deceptively normal 130–180 mg/dL. Providers trained to equate DKA with a glucose in the 400s can miss it entirely.

The practical takeaways: hold SGLT2 inhibitors before elective surgery per current guidance (traditionally 3–4 days, though emerging evidence questions whether shorter holds suffice), ask specifically about SGLT2-inhibitor use during the preoperative workup since patients don't always volunteer it, and if a postoperative patient develops unexplained nausea, tachypnea, or malaise, check a basic metabolic panel and ketones or pH even when the fingerstick glucose looks reassuring. Treatment mirrors standard DKA management — volume resuscitation, insulin, and dextrose supplementation to permit insulin dosing without causing hypoglycemia — despite the near-normal starting glucose.

🕰️This Week in Anesthesia History
August 17, 1847

Ten months after ether's first public surgical demonstration in Boston, the new practice of inhalation anesthesia was already spreading through Europe's medical and social circles. On the evening of August 17, 1847, the Danish writer Hans Christian Andersen dined at the Edinburgh home of physician James Young Simpson, where the guests experimented with ether inhalation as an after-dinner curiosity. Andersen later wrote that he found the spectacle "distasteful, especially to see ladies in this dreamy intoxication," even as he acknowledged ether as "a wonderful and blessed invention to use in painful operations." The dinner sits at an interesting hinge point in anesthesia's history: barely three months later, Simpson would identify chloroform's anesthetic properties in his own home, and by early 1848 he was arguing publicly for its use in obstetrics — a debate that would occupy the next decade of anesthetic practice far more than ether's parlor-trick phase ever did.

🧠Board Question of the Week

A 29-year-old woman received spinal anesthesia with bupivacaine for a cesarean delivery three days ago and now reports a severe, throbbing headache that is 10/10 when upright and nearly resolves when supine, accompanied by mild neck stiffness but no fever. Conservative measures — fluids, caffeine, and analgesics — have not helped after 24 hours. Which of the following is the most appropriate next step?

Correct answer: B — Epidural blood patch is the definitive treatment once conservative therapy fails

The clinical picture — a positional headache dramatically worse upright and improved supine, developing within days of dural puncture, without fever — is classic for postdural puncture headache (PDPH). Ongoing CSF leak through the dural defect lowers intracranial pressure and causes traction on pain-sensitive meninges when upright. Conservative measures are reasonable first-line steps, but once they fail after 24–48 hours, an epidural blood patch — injecting 15–20 mL of the patient's own blood into the epidural space near the puncture site to form a clot that seals the leak — is the definitive treatment, effective in roughly 70–90% of cases after a single patch.

A is a reasonable consideration if the headache doesn't fit the classic positional pattern or red flags emerge, but it isn't the next step for a textbook-positional PDPH that has already failed conservative therapy. C jumps to treating meningitis without supporting signs — no fever, and the positional quality argues strongly against it — though this week's headlines are a reminder that contaminated neuraxial injectate can cause genuine meningitis, that presentation doesn't fit a purely positional, afebrile headache. D under-treats a patient who has already failed conservative management and is suffering a debilitating, functionally disabling headache.
