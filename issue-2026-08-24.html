📰Headlines

Bupivacaine-Contamination Recall Widens to a Fourth Kit Manufacturer as Medline Issues Correction

On August 21, the FDA disclosed a Class I recall — its most serious category — covering Medline convenience kits that pair B. Braun components with Huons Co. bupivacaine hydrochloride in dextrose injection, the same contaminated lot at the center of the B. Braun, Arrow International, and Becton Dickinson corrections this newsletter has tracked since spring (our July 27 and August 17 issues). Medline notified affected customers on June 8 to quarantine the kits and apply over-labels removing the recalled bupivacaine component before continued use; as of a June 19 update, the company had logged no additional serious injuries beyond the 35 B. Braun had already reported. The repeating pattern — one contaminated drug lot rippling through kit after kit for months — is a reminder that "recalled drug, unaffected device" corrections can lag badly at the periphery of a distribution network, and programs should keep checking incoming spinal and epidural trays against the FDA's lot list rather than assuming last month's correction closed the loop.

FDA Flags a Class I Recall for Abiomed's 14Fr Introducer Kits Over Clot Risk

On August 3, the FDA classified a recall of Abiomed's 14Fr Low Profile Introducer Kits as Class I after the company identified that prolonged indwelling use of the introducer can promote thrombus formation at the insertion site, with the potential for embolization, vessel occlusion, or loss of vascular access during high-acuity cardiac support. The kits are most often placed for Impella heart-pump insertion, putting cardiac anesthesiology and CT surgery teams squarely in the path of the alert; the FDA paired the notice in the same week's bulletin with the already-familiar ResMed Astral ventilator recall (our August 17 issue), a reminder that much of this summer's device-safety attention has centered on prolonged life-support hardware rather than routine OR equipment. Facilities holding affected lots should review Abiomed's field correction for monitoring and exchange guidance before further prolonged placements.

AANA Launches a Patient-Facing Anesthesia Education Site

On August 22, AANA unveiled Anesthesia Answers, a new consumer-facing resource at AnesthesiaAnswers.org built by CRNAs to walk patients through what to expect before, during, and after anesthesia for surgery, childbirth, or a procedure. The stated goal is to arm patients with plain-language, evidence-based information ahead of their preoperative conversation with an anesthesia provider, rather than leaving them to sort through internet search results of uneven quality — a resource gap this newsletter's own readers will likely field questions about at the bedside. It's a modest launch, but a useful bookmark: a lay-language explainer patients can be pointed to the same day they learn they're getting anesthesia.

📚From the Literature

A Meta-Analysis Says the 2-Hour Clear-Fluid Fast Doesn't Need to Be 2 Hours in Kids

A systematic review and meta-analysis in the British Journal of Anaesthesia examined whether shortening the preoperative clear-fluid fasting window to one hour or less — instead of the traditional two-hour minimum — raises aspiration risk in elective pediatric procedures. Across the pooled cohort, the shorter fast was not associated with any increase in pulmonary aspiration, and it tracked with reduced postoperative nausea, thirst, and hunger scores compared with the traditional window. The authors frame the finding as support for "sip-til-send" protocols already gaining traction in pediatric preop units, and it lines up with the physiology this issue's Knowledge Pearl walks through: clear liquids empty from the stomach fast enough that the extra hour was mostly doing nothing but making kids miserable.

Dabke, T., Scott, T. E., & Taylor, B. (2026). Impact of preoperative fasting duration on perioperative complications in elective paediatric procedures: A systematic review and meta-analysis. British Journal of Anaesthesia, 136(1), 158–166. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.bja.2025.10.037

⚖️Legislation & Scope Tracker

DC Regulators Strip "Nurse Anesthesiologist" From Proposed CRNA Title Rules

The District of Columbia's Department of Health dropped the term "nurse anesthesiologist" from proposed regulations governing CRNA titling after ASA, the AMA, and the DC Society of Anesthesiologists formally opposed the language, arguing it risked confusing patients about who is administering their anesthesia. ASA publicized the outcome in June as a win in the long-running title fight that also produced AANA's decision to withdraw its federal trademark applications for the same phrase after a roughly two-year opposition proceeding (our July 13 issue) — two separate venues, same underlying dispute, both resolved against the "nurse anesthesiologist" label for now. CRNAs in DC keep their scope of practice; what changes is only what the credential can be called in state rulemaking.

ASA · ASA Applauds DC Department of Health's Rejection of "Misleading" Nurse Anesthesiologist Phrase

Ohio's New "Collaboration" Law Complicates the State's Own Federal Opt-Out

When Ohio notified CMS it no longer needed the federal physician-supervision rule for CRNAs (our July 20 issue), it looked like straightforward momentum toward independent CRNA practice. House Bill 52 — signed by Governor Mike DeWine on March 10 and in effect since June 8 — tells a more complicated story: it replaces the old physician-supervision requirement with a "collaboration" model, but the fine print still requires a collaborating physician, dentist, or podiatrist to be physically present in the facility for the induction, maintenance, and emergence of general anesthesia, and adds a new mandatory disclosure telling patients by name who is administering their anesthesia. Unsurprisingly, ASA and AANA have described the same law in opposite terms — ASA calling it a preservation of "physician-led anesthesia care," AANA calling it a "modernized" CRNA practice law — a reminder that scope-of-practice fights increasingly turn on how supervision gets defined on paper, not simply whether it's required at all.

💡Knowledge Pearl

Clear liquids don't sit in the stomach the way solids do — that's the whole basis for "sip-til-send." Clear, non-particulate liquids (water, pulp-free juice, black coffee, sports drinks) empty from the stomach largely by first-order kinetics driven by volume and hydrostatic pressure, with a half-emptying time typically under 15 minutes regardless of whether the starting volume was 150 mL or 450 mL — residual volume converges back toward a low fasting baseline well within an hour for most patients. That's mechanistically different from solids, fats, and protein-containing liquids like milk, which trigger antral grinding, pyloric sieving, and duodenal feedback inhibition that can stretch gastric emptying out past four to six hours. ASA and European (ESAIC) guidance has permitted clear liquids up to two hours before elective anesthesia for years; the data in this issue's Literature pick is pushing programs to ask whether even that two-hour buffer is doing much beyond making patients thirstier — particularly in pediatrics, where dehydration and hypoglycemia from prolonged fasting are a bigger day-to-day problem than a hypothetical aspiration the volume-and-timing data doesn't support.

Board-relevant distinction: know the classic 2-4-6-8 fasting rule (clears–breast milk–infant formula/light meal–solids/full meal), but also know why clear liquids get the shortest window — NPO status is fundamentally a volume-and-acidity risk model, and clear liquids fail to accumulate stomach volume the way solids do. On an exam, don't conflate "recent PO intake" (a genuine aspiration risk factor regardless of what any guideline says) with "guideline non-compliance" (missed the stated fasting window) — the former calls for a full-stomach precaution; the latter, in an otherwise low-risk elective case, often doesn't.

🕰️This Week in Anesthesia History
August 29, 1809

On August 29, 1809, Oliver Wendell Holmes Sr. was born in Cambridge, Massachusetts — the physician, essayist, and Harvard anatomy professor who, in a November 21, 1846 letter to William T. G. Morton, proposed naming the new phenomenon "anaesthesia," from the Greek for "without sensation," and further suggested "anaesthetic" for the agent and "to anaesthetize" for the act of administering it. Morton had given his first public ether demonstration barely five weeks earlier, on October 16, 1846, in the same city Holmes called home — the operating theater now preserved as the Ether Dome at Massachusetts General Hospital, walking distance from where this year's AANA Congress crowds gathered this week (see Headlines, above). Holmes's proposed vocabulary stuck almost immediately and has outlived nearly everything else written about ether in 1846.

🧠Board Question of the Week

A 68-year-old man undergoing an interscalene brachial plexus block with 30 mL of 0.5% bupivacaine becomes acutely agitated, then develops perioral numbness and tinnitus, followed roughly 90 seconds later by a witnessed generalized tonic-clonic seizure. Shortly afterward, the monitor shows a wide-complex tachycardia. In addition to airway management and benzodiazepines for seizure control, which of the following is the most appropriate immediate treatment?

Correct answer: B — 20% lipid emulsion is first-line treatment for local anesthetic systemic toxicity

The sequence — agitation and perioral numbness, then tinnitus, then seizure, then cardiovascular collapse — is the textbook progression of local anesthetic systemic toxicity (LAST) following inadvertent intravascular uptake of a large-volume amide block. Per ASRA's LAST checklist, 20% lipid emulsion should be given promptly alongside airway management and seizure control: a bolus (roughly 1.5 mL/kg lean body mass over about a minute), repeated once or twice for persistent cardiovascular instability, followed by a continuous infusion. The mechanism is thought to involve both a "lipid sink" that pulls lipophilic local anesthetic out of cardiac tissue and improved delivery of fatty acids to stressed myocardial mitochondria.

A is wrong because lidocaine is itself an amide local anesthetic that competes at the same sodium channels and can worsen LAST rather than treat it. C is wrong because ASRA guidance specifically recommends reducing standard ACLS epinephrine doses (to roughly 1 mcg/kg or less) in LAST, since full-dose epinephrine can impair resuscitation with lipid emulsion and provoke more malignant arrhythmias in a heart already destabilized by sodium-channel blockade. D treats hyperkalemia or calcium-channel-blocker toxicity, not LAST, and has no established role here.
