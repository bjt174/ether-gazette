📰Headlines

FDA Investigates Sevoflurane After Reports of Fatal Neurologic Complications Tied to a Rare Genetic Variant

On July 2, the FDA alerted clinicians that it is investigating sevoflurane and other volatile anesthetics following published reports of "unexpected catastrophic outcomes" — severe neurologic injury and death — in adult and pediatric patients of maternal Venezuelan ancestry after routine general anesthesia. A rare mitochondrial variant, MT-ND4 m.11232T>C, has turned up in a subset of the affected patients, and the agency notes the concern could extend to volatile anesthetics as a class, not sevoflurane alone. The alert follows a joint ASA/Society for Pediatric Anesthesia notice issued January 27 flagging the same pattern. For now, the FDA advises clinicians caring for patients of Venezuelan maternal lineage to consider intravenous or regional techniques over volatile agents where clinically appropriate, and to report suspected cases through MedWatch.

More Than 80% of Surgery Centers Now Plan to Staff Anesthesia Through Contractors, Not Employees

A new Becker's ASC survey of ambulatory surgery center leaders finds independent-contractor arrangements — 44% with a stipend, 36% without — have become the dominant anesthesia staffing model for 2026, together outpacing traditional direct-employment coverage, which only 9% of leaders still expect to use. Leaders point to deepening workforce shortages, rising compensation demands, and reimbursement that hasn't kept pace, forcing many centers to subsidize coverage that used to be cost-neutral. The same survey finds ASCs increasingly leaning on CRNA-only and hybrid care-team models to keep rooms running, with certified anesthesiologist assistants picking up a growing share of that work as more states open CAA practice pathways.

📚From the Literature

A Nerve Block Before Hip Fracture Surgery May Protect the Heart, Not Just Control Pain

A two-center retrospective cohort study of 1,467 older adults with high cardiac risk undergoing hip fracture surgery found that adding a single-injection peripheral nerve block to general or neuraxial anesthesia was associated with a lower rate of postoperative myocardial injury than anesthesia without a block. The authors' leading explanation isn't purely analgesic: uncontrolled pain drives sympathetic activation and higher cardiac workload, so better pain control from the block may blunt the physiologic stress that leads to perioperative myocardial injury (MINS) in a population that often can't tolerate it. For students, it's a useful nudge to think of regional techniques as cardiac risk mitigation, not just an opioid-sparing add-on — particularly in exactly the frail, high-risk population hip fracture patients represent. As a retrospective cohort study, it shows association, not proof of causation; a prospective trial would be needed to confirm the effect.

Zhang, H., Wu, J., Ni, D., Sun, Y., Ren, Y., Miao, Y., Jia, N., Zhang, Y., Qu, Z., Wang, D.-X., & Hua, Z. (2026). Association of peripheral nerve block with postoperative myocardial injury in high-risk cardiac older adults undergoing hip fracture surgery: A two-center retrospective cohort study. Anesthesiology, 144(5), 1094–1104. https://doi.org/10.1097/ALN.0000000000005921

⚖️Legislation & Scope Tracker

California's AB 876 Quietly Takes Effect, Codifying What CRNAs Already Do

Effective January 1, 2026, AB 876 — chaptered last October and sponsored by the California Association of Nurse Anesthesiology — added new sections to the state's Business and Professions Code formally defining "anesthesia services" and confirming that a patient-specific anesthesia order from a physician, dentist, or podiatrist fully authorizes a CRNA's care, including medication selection and administration, without that selection counting as a prescription. The law is explicitly declarative rather than expansive: it doesn't broaden what CRNAs may do, but it closes ambiguity that had left California's anesthesia-services framework resting mostly on a 2012 appellate decision rather than statute.

Davis Wright Tremaine · California's 2026 APRN Practice Reforms: What AB 583 and AB 876 Mean for NPs, CRNAs, and Healthcare Facilities

Florida's Autonomous-Practice Bill Dies in Committee — But CRNAs Say It'll Be Back

HB 375, which would have let qualified CRNAs register as autonomous advanced practice registered nurses and drop the state's onsite-physician-medical-direction requirement, died in Senate Rules on March 13 without a floor vote. Facilities would still have been free to impose their own supervision policies under the bill, but the statutory mandate would have gone away statewide. With 29 states plus D.C. now allowing CRNA practice without physician supervision, Florida's proponents are already framing this session's outcome as a setback rather than a defeat, and expect a refiled version next year.

💡Knowledge Pearl

Why an unmasked mitochondrial disorder can turn a routine general anesthetic catastrophic. This week's FDA alert on sevoflurane is a sharp reminder that volatile anesthetics aren't metabolically inert. Mitochondria generate the ATP that excitable, oxygen-hungry tissue — brain and heart chief among it — depends on minute to minute, and volatile agents are known to mildly suppress mitochondrial electron transport chain activity as part of how they produce anesthesia. In a patient with a normal mitochondrial reserve, that suppression is trivial and fully reversible. In a patient carrying a pathogenic mitochondrial variant — like the MT-ND4 m.11232T>C mutation implicated in the current cases — baseline oxidative capacity is already reduced, and volatile-agent suppression on top of that can tip vulnerable tissue into energy failure, producing exactly the picture being reported: unexplained neurologic devastation or cardiac collapse in patients with no other risk factors.

The practical takeaway for boards and bedside alike isn't "avoid volatiles in everyone" — it's recognizing when mitochondrial disease is on the differential (unexplained lactic acidosis, myopathy, prior anesthetic complications, suggestive family or ancestry history) and defaulting toward total intravenous or regional techniques in those patients, since propofol and regional anesthesia don't carry the same electron-transport-chain liability at clinical doses.

🕰️This Week in Anesthesia History
July 25, 1894

Harold Griffith was born in Montreal on this date — the anesthetist who, on January 23, 1942, gave the world its first practical neuromuscular blocking agent. Working with resident Enid Johnson at the Homeopathic Hospital of Montreal, Griffith injected an extract of curare, Intocostrin, into a 20-year-old undergoing an appendectomy, producing profound muscle relaxation without deepening the ether anesthesia already in progress. Before that afternoon, adequate relaxation for abdominal surgery meant anesthetizing patients dangerously deep; Griffith's report, published later that year, opened the door to lighter, safer anesthesia paired with deliberate paralysis — the conceptual split between hypnosis, analgesia, and relaxation that still organizes how anesthesia is taught today. Griffith went on to introduce cyclopropane to Canadian practice, establish the country's first postoperative recovery room, and serve as founding president of the World Federation of Societies of Anaesthesiologists.

🧠Board Question of the Week

In the PACU, a patient extubated 20 minutes ago after a laparoscopic case involving rocuronium develops intermittent upper-airway obstruction, uncoordinated jerky limb movements, and difficulty maintaining a sustained head lift. A qualitative peripheral nerve stimulator shows a train-of-four with no fade appreciable to the observer. Which statement best describes this scenario?

Correct answer: B — Qualitative TOF can look reassuring while real residual blockade persists

The vignette describes classic signs of residual neuromuscular blockade: upper-airway obstruction, uncoordinated movement, and inability to sustain a head lift — a bedside surrogate for a train-of-four ratio below roughly 0.9, the threshold below which pharyngeal function and airway protection are impaired. The trap in this question is the "normal" qualitative TOF: the human eye reliably detects fade only when the train-of-four ratio has already dropped well below 0.4, so a qualitative twitch monitor can look unremarkable at ratios between roughly 0.4 and 0.9 — precisely the range where clinically meaningful residual paralysis lives. This gap is the rationale behind APSF's ongoing push for quantitative neuromuscular monitoring (which reports an actual numeric TOF ratio) as the more reliable standard before extubation.

Emergence delirium doesn't produce upper-airway obstruction or an inability to sustain a head lift as its primary features, so C is a weaker fit for this clinical picture. D repeats the same qualitative-versus-quantitative trap as A — a "normal" TOF by qualitative assessment never fully excludes clinically important residual blockade, whereas a quantitative TOF ratio at or above 0.9 does.
