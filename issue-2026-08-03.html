📰Headlines

A Federal Judge Blocks New Loan Limits for Nursing Students Days Before They Were Set to Take Effect

On June 25, U.S. District Judge Beryl Howell issued a preliminary injunction blocking a Department of Education rule that would have redefined "professional degree" programs to exclude nursing — including nurse anesthesia — from the higher federal loan limits available to law, medicine, and other professional-track students. Filed by the American Association of Nurse Practitioners, the PA Education Association, and other trade groups, the suit argued the Department exceeded its statutory authority; Howell agreed the plaintiffs were likely to succeed on the merits. Had the rule taken effect July 1 as scheduled, graduate nursing students — DNP and CRNA students among them — would have seen annual borrowing capped at $20,500 (aggregate $100,000), instead of the $50,000-per-year, $200,000-aggregate ceiling available to "professional degree" programs. The injunction runs alongside, but is separate from, the AANA-joined "Nurse-Forward" lawsuit filed May 29 challenging the same underlying rule; both cases continue while litigation plays out.

ABC News · Nursing Gains 'Professional' Label for Student Loans After Judge's Ruling

Sixth Circuit Rules AANA Lacks Standing to Challenge Insurers Over CRNA Reimbursement Rates

On July 21, the U.S. Court of Appeals for the Sixth Circuit ruled against AANA in its case seeking to compel HHS enforcement of the ACA's provider-nondiscrimination provision — the rule barring insurers from paying CRNAs less than physicians for identical services solely based on license type. The court didn't reach the merits of the discrimination claim; it held instead that AANA lacked associational standing to sue on its members' behalf. AANA President Jeff Molter was pointed in response: "This decision does not validate the behavior of insurance companies," he said, adding that below-parity reimbursement "remain[s] unlawful under current statute." With the federal appellate avenue narrowed, AANA says it will lean on state-level advocacy and look for other federal pathways to press the reimbursement fight.

PR Newswire · Statement from AANA President Jeff Molter on the Sixth Circuit Decision

Bipartisan Pushback Grows Against Medicare Contractors' Plan to Stop Covering Nerve Blocks for Chronic Pain

Five Medicare Administrative Contractors have proposed local coverage determinations that would deny payment for peripheral nerve blocks used to treat chronic pain — procedures regional anesthesiologists and CRNAs perform routinely as an opioid-sparing alternative. A coalition led by ASA, ASRA Pain Medicine, IPSIS, and AAPM&R is fighting the proposals, and 25 bipartisan members of Congress, led by Reps. Brittany Pettersen and Claudia Tenney, wrote CMS warning that eliminating coverage "could result in a spike in opioid use and misuse." The proposed LCDs remain unfinalized; opponents are pushing for outright withdrawal rather than a narrowed version, arguing that even a scaled-back policy would strand patients who rely on nerve blocks as a non-opioid option.

Becker's ASC · What Happened to the Nerve Block Coverage Denial? Where the Fight Stands Now

Propofol Shortage Persists as Dr. Reddy's Exits the Market

Dr. Reddy's Laboratories discontinued its propofol emulsion injection line in early 2026, according to its own SEC filings, deepening a shortage that has now stretched into its fourth year on the ASHP tracker. Fresenius Kabi's Diprivan remains the most consistent branded source, with Amneal, Avenacy, and Avet supplying generic vials on a rolling, often-allocated basis. ASHP continues to list propofol as an active national shortage, and pharmacy leaders are advising anesthesia departments to keep contingency plans — etomidate, ketamine, or a volatile induction where appropriate — current rather than assuming steady vial availability through the fall.

📚From the Literature

The Study Behind the FDA's Sevoflurane Alert: How One Mitochondrial Variant Turns a Routine Anesthetic Catastrophic

Following up on the FDA's July 2 alert (covered in our July 27 issue), the Anesthesiology paper behind it identifies a mechanism: patients carrying the mitochondrial DNA variant m.11232T>C — concentrated along a specific Venezuelan maternal lineage — carry a leucine-to-proline substitution in Complex I of the electron transport chain. In vitro, sevoflurane exposure in cells carrying the variant sharply suppressed mitochondrial oxygen consumption, with the effect concentrated on Complex I–dependent respiration; propofol exposure did not produce the same suppression. That distinction is the clinically actionable part — it's a volatile-agent-specific vulnerability, not a blanket anesthesia risk, which is why total intravenous anesthesia is emerging as the primary risk-mitigation strategy for patients on the affected differential, rather than avoiding anesthesia altogether.

⚖️Legislation & Scope Tracker

Rhode Island Weighs Removing Physician Oversight for CRNAs

House Bill H 7426, introduced in January and still pending before the Rhode Island General Assembly, would let CRNAs determine the scope of their own anesthesia practice within licensed hospitals, clinics, and offices, replacing current oversight language, with an effective date of January 1, 2027 if enacted. A companion measure, S 2111, is moving in the Senate. Rhode Island would join the majority of states that have already dropped mandatory physician oversight of CRNA practice if either bill reaches the governor's desk.

Rhode Island General Assembly · 2026 — H 7426

The National Scorecard: Where Supervision Opt-Outs and Independent Practice Stand Now

As of this year, 25 states plus Guam have formally opted out of the federal Medicare requirement that a physician supervise CRNA-administered anesthesia, while 29 states plus Washington, D.C. now permit CRNAs to practice without physician supervision under their own nurse practice acts — two related but legally distinct tallies that are easy to conflate. The opt-out count governs Medicare billing; the practice-act count governs what a CRNA may actually do day to day, and individual facility policy can still impose supervision requirements even where state law doesn't require it.

💡Knowledge Pearl

Recognizing and treating Local Anesthetic Systemic Toxicity (LAST) when a block goes wrong. LAST results from unintentional intravascular injection or excessive systemic absorption of local anesthetic, producing a spectrum from perioral numbness and tinnitus to seizures and, in the most severe cases, cardiovascular collapse. Cardiac arrest can be the presenting sign with bupivacaine specifically, because its high protein binding and lipophilicity let it linger in cardiac tissue and block sodium channels longer than lidocaine does. CNS warning signs may be masked entirely under general anesthesia or heavy sedation, so cardiovascular instability out of proportion to the clinical picture should raise suspicion for LAST on its own.

Treatment starts with stopping the injection, calling for help, and securing the airway with 100% oxygen — preventing the acidosis and hypoxia that worsen toxicity — followed immediately by 20% lipid emulsion: a 1.5 mL/kg bolus, then a continuous infusion, per the current ASRA checklist. Avoid vasopressin, calcium channel blockers, and beta blockers, which ASRA's guideline flags as harmful in this setting, and be prepared for a prolonged resuscitation — LAST-related cardiac arrest can require chest compressions well past the usual ACLS endpoints while the lipid emulsion takes effect.

🕰️This Week in Anesthesia History
August 9, 1819

William Thomas Green Morton, the Boston dentist who gave the world its first successful public demonstration of surgical anesthesia, was born in Charlton, Massachusetts. Twenty-seven years later, on October 16, 1846, Morton administered ether to patient Edward Gilbert Abbott in the Bulfinch Building amphitheater at Massachusetts General Hospital — now nicknamed the "Ether Dome" — while surgeon John Collins Warren removed a vascular tumor from Abbott's neck. Warren's reported remark to the assembled crowd, "Gentlemen, this is no humbug," marked the moment surgical pain stopped being an unavoidable cost of survival. Morton spent much of his later life in patent disputes over the discovery — he'd tried to market ether anesthesia commercially under the name "Letheon" — and died largely impoverished in 1868, but the October demonstration remains the date the profession marks as anesthesia's public birth, with Morton's August birthday a quieter footnote in the same story.

🧠Board Question of the Week

A 34-year-old man with a T6 spinal cord injury sustained three weeks ago presents for incision and drainage of a sacral abscess. The surgeon requests rapid-sequence induction. Which of the following neuromuscular blocking agent choices is most appropriate, and why?

Correct answer: B — High-dose rocuronium matches succinylcholine's speed without the hyperkalemia risk

Denervation injuries — spinal cord injury, burns, prolonged immobility, stroke, Guillain-Barré syndrome — trigger proliferation of extrajunctional acetylcholine receptors across the affected muscle membrane. Succinylcholine's depolarization of that expanded receptor field can release enough potassium to cause cardiac arrest, with risk beginning around 24–72 hours post-injury and persisting for many months until the receptor upregulation resolves. Rocuronium at 1.2 mg/kg achieves an onset (roughly 60 seconds) comparable to succinylcholine, making it the standard substitute for RSI in this population.

C is wrong because spinal cord injury produces the same extrajunctional receptor proliferation below the level of injury as peripheral denervation does — the risk isn't limited to peripheral nerve damage. D's premise is simply false: rocuronium, not cisatracurium, is the fastest-onset non-depolarizing agent, which is exactly why rocuronium — not cisatracurium — is the go-to RSI substitute when succinylcholine is contraindicated.
