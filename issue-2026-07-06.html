📰Headlines

Two Class I Anesthesia Workstation Recalls Put Ventilator Power Failures in the Spotlight

The FDA's most serious recall category now covers two mainstream anesthesia machine lines at once. GE HealthCare's Carestation 600/700 series can shut down unexpectedly and reboot to factory defaults if AC mains power is lost and restored — silently dropping ventilation settings mid-case. Separately, Dräger expanded a correction for its Atlan A350/A350XL workstations after a manufacturing defect in the piston ventilator was found capable of causing ventilation failure, sometimes flagged only by a terse "Ventilator error!!!" message. Neither manufacturer has reported patient injury, but both now instruct clinicians to keep a self-inflating bag at hand and to treat any power event or ventilator alarm as a cue to switch to manual ventilation immediately.

CMS's 2026 Fee Schedule Nets Anesthesiologists a Bare 0.88 Percent Bump

The finalized CY 2026 Medicare Physician Fee Schedule sets the anesthesia conversion factor at $20.4976 — up just 0.88 percent from 2025, and $20.5998 for clinicians in Advanced Alternative Payment Models. ASA notes that a 2.5 percent statutory increase from last year's budget bill was largely offset by a new -2.5 percent "efficiency adjustment" CMS applied to non-time-based services, plus a roughly 7 percent cut to facility-based payments (partly offset by a 4 percent bump for non-facility settings). Advocacy groups call the net effect a real-terms pay cut once inflation is factored in.

Cal State Fullerton's Nurse Anesthesia Program Climbs to No. 3 Nationally

U.S. News & World Report's 2026 Best Graduate Schools rankings move Cal State Fullerton's nurse anesthesia program — run jointly with Kaiser Permanente's School of Anesthesia — from No. 7 to No. 3 in the country, and No. 1 in California, tying UNC Chapel Hill. Program leadership credits the depth of the Kaiser Permanente clinical partnership for the jump, a reminder of how much a strong clinical-site relationship can shape a program's trajectory.

📚From the Literature

New Guidance Simplifies Genetic Testing for Malignant Hyperthermia Risk

A special article proposes a practical, three-question screen clinicians can use before deciding whether a patient needs formal malignant hyperthermia (MH) genetic workup: has the patient had an MH reaction or known susceptibility; does a family member have MH susceptibility or a reaction history; and has the patient or a relative already been genetically tested, with what result. Sequencing of RYR1, CACNA1S, and STAC3 remains the backbone of confirmatory testing, but the authors are candid that a negative panel does not fully clear a patient — "no patient can ever be at zero risk" — so relatives of confirmed cases and patients with high-risk variants should still be managed as MH-susceptible even without a personal reaction history.

Riazi, S., Watt, S., Plester, J., & Biesecker, L. G. (2026). A proposed North American approach for genetic testing of individuals at risk for malignant hyperthermia. Anesthesiology, 144(4), 776–783. https://doi.org/10.1097/ALN.0000000000005849

Florida's HB 375 Dies in the Senate — Autonomous CRNA Practice Waits Another Year

The bill we flagged last week as "eyeing a July 1 effective date" didn't make it. HB 375 passed the Florida House 78–28 on February 4, but its Senate companion, SB 462, died in the Health Policy Committee, and HB 375 itself died in the Senate Rules Committee on March 13 without a floor vote. The measure would have let qualified CRNAs register as autonomous advanced practice registered nurses and practice without physician oversight; supporters are expected to refile next session, following the same path as predecessor bills HB 649 (died 2025) and HB 257 (died 2024).

The National Count Holds at 37 Opt-Out States Plus D.C.

With Florida's push stalled for another cycle, the national scope-of-practice map is effectively unchanged this week: 37 states plus the District of Columbia have opted out of the federal Medicare physician-supervision requirement, though roughly 29 states plus D.C. pair that opt-out with true full, independent state-law practice authority. The gap between the two — a federal billing waiver versus an actual state-law grant of independence — is exactly the terrain bills like HB 375 keep trying, and failing, to close.

💡Knowledge Pearl

Dantrolene works upstream of the calcium overload that drives malignant hyperthermia. MH is triggered by volatile anesthetics or succinylcholine in genetically susceptible patients (most often via RYR1 mutations), causing the ryanodine receptor on the sarcoplasmic reticulum to dump calcium into the muscle cytoplasm uncontrollably. That flood of calcium drives sustained contraction, runaway heat production, and skyrocketing oxygen consumption — the hypermetabolic state that produces the classic triad of rising end-tidal CO2, muscle rigidity, and fever.

Dantrolene binds the RYR1 receptor directly and blocks calcium release from the sarcoplasmic reticulum, cutting off the process at its source rather than just treating the downstream fever or acidosis. Initial dosing is 2.5 mg/kg IV, repeated every 5–10 minutes until symptoms resolve — there is no fixed maximum in an ongoing crisis. Because a single reaction doesn't fully rule a patient out or in, current guidance leans on a short screening history (personal or family MH reaction, and any prior genetic testing) to decide who actually needs formal RYR1/CACNA1S/STAC3 sequencing, rather than testing everyone with a vague family rumor of "anesthesia problems."

🕰️This Week in Anesthesia History
June 17, 1931

On June 17, 1931, Agatha Hodgins founded the National Association of Nurse Anesthetists — the organization that would become today's AANA — in Cleveland, Ohio. Hodgins, who had already helped establish nurse anesthesia training at Lakeside Hospital and co-founded the Lakeside Alumnae Association of Nurse Anesthetists a decade earlier, built the national association specifically to defend nurse anesthetists' right to practice against a wave of early-20th-century legal challenges arguing that administering anesthesia constituted the unlicensed practice of medicine. Nearly a century later, the fight over who gets to define and defend that scope of practice — see this week's Legislation Tracker — is still very much alive.

🧠Board Question of the Week

Per the ASA's 2026 practice guideline, a patient undergoing an open abdominal procedure receives a bilateral transversus abdominis plane (TAP) block as part of a multimodal analgesia plan. Which of the following best describes what this fascial plane block actually accomplishes?

Correct answer: B — Somatic sensory afferents of the anterior abdominal wall

A TAP block deposits local anesthetic in the fascial plane between the internal oblique and transversus abdominis muscles, where the somatic sensory nerves supplying the anterior abdominal wall (roughly T6–L1) travel. It reliably reduces incisional (somatic) pain and opioid consumption in the first 24 postoperative hours — exactly the outcome the 2026 ASA guideline's "strong" recommendation is based on — but it does not touch visceral pain from the bowel, peritoneum, or other organs, which travels via separate autonomic pathways.

A is wrong because visceral afferents aren't in the TAP plane at all, which is why patients still need systemic multimodal analgesia for deep visceral discomfort. C describes neuraxial (spinal/epidural) sympathetic blockade, not a peripheral fascial plane block. D is incorrect — the phrenic nerve runs nowhere near the TAP plane; diaphragmatic irritation from pneumoperitoneum isn't addressed by this block.
