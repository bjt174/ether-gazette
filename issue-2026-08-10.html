📰Headlines

CMS Finalizes 2026 Pay Rates, and Anesthesiologists Get a Fraction of the Raise Congress Promised

On October 31, 2025, CMS released its Calendar Year 2026 Medicare Physician Fee Schedule final rule, setting two separate anesthesia conversion factors for the first time: $20.5998 per unit for clinicians in qualifying Advanced Alternative Payment Models — a 1.39% increase over 2025 — and $20.4976 for everyone else, up just 0.88%. The One Big Beautiful Bill Act had written a flat 2.5% statutory bump into the broader physician fee schedule, but CMS's separate adjustments to practice-expense and other methodology inputs absorbed most of that gain before it reached anesthesia providers specifically. ASA has pressed CMS on the shortfall since the rule dropped; it took effect January 1 and now governs claims through the rest of 2026.

ASA · CMS Finalizes Policies Undermining Anesthesia Payments

ASA's New Perioperative Pain Guideline Puts Fascial Plane Blocks at the Center of Routine Practice

ASA published its 2026 Practice Guideline on Perioperative Pain Management Using Local and Regional Analgesia for Cardiothoracic, Mastectomy, and Abdominal Surgeries in the January issue of Anesthesiology. For adults, the Task Force strongly recommends fascial plane blocks — erector spinae, transversus abdominis plane, PECS, and similar techniques — to reduce pain and opioid requirements in the first 24 postoperative hours after open cardiothoracic, abdominal, retroperitoneal, or pelvic surgery and mastectomy, with a conditional recommendation extending to minimally invasive abdominal and cardiothoracic cases and open hernia repair. For children, the guideline strongly recommends fascial plane blocks after open cardiac or thoracic surgery. The document formalizes what many programs already do informally, arriving just as payers separately scrutinize block reimbursement (see this week's Literature pick, below).

Anesthesiology · 2026 ASA Practice Guideline on Perioperative Pain Management

FDA's Class I Recall of Medline Anesthesia Circuit Kits Remains in Effect

The FDA's most serious recall category still applies to Medline's Anesthesia Circuit Kits and standalone Anesthesia Circuits containing 120-inch expandable tubing, first flagged in a January 16 alert. Affected units — 27 SKUs across lot numbers beginning 25E, 25F, 25G, and 25H — can develop cracks that leak during use, risking ventilation failure, hypoxia, hypoventilation, and inadequate anesthetic delivery. Medline is asking facilities to check inventory against the affected lot numbers, quarantine and destroy any matches, and notify downstream recipients; departments that haven't reconciled circuit stock against the list since January should treat this as a standing task, not a closed one.

FDA · Anesthesia Kit Recall: Medline Removes Anesthesia Circuits and Circuit Kits

Etomidate Shortage Deepens as Manufacturers Cite Surging Demand

Etomidate injection has slid further into shortage, with multiple formulations listed unavailable or on backorder as of a July 20 update; manufacturers point to a broad increase in demand rather than a manufacturing failure. One formulation now carries an estimated recovery date of March 2027. The drug's appeal as a hemodynamically stable induction agent for unstable or cardiac patients makes the timing awkward — propofol supply remains constrained too (see this week's Drug Shortage Watch), narrowing the induction-agent bench precisely when programs most need options.

📚From the Literature

Nerve Blocks Ease PACU Pain but May Worsen It Later, Large Retrospective Cohort Finds

A single-center retrospective cohort of 22,956 adults undergoing orthopaedic surgery with general anesthesia at Massachusetts General Hospital (2016–2020) found that a peripheral nerve block was associated with lower maximum pain scores and less opioid use in the PACU — but higher maximum pain scores and roughly 23% more opioid consumption over the rest of the hospital stay. The authors point to rebound pain, the well-described phenomenon in which analgesia collapses sharply as a single-shot block wears off, as the likely driver. The findings don't argue against blocks — the PACU-phase benefit was real — but they complicate the simple "regional is opioid-sparing" framing at the center of the ongoing Medicare nerve-block coverage fight (our August 3 issue), and they're a reminder to plan the handoff from block to systemic analgesia as deliberately as the block itself.

Chung, A. R., Mather, R. V., Gutierrez, R., Liu, R., Leung, C. F. A., Zhang, M., Santa Cruz Mercado, L. A., Houle, T. T., Bittner, E. A., & Purdon, P. L. (2025). Association of peripheral nerve blocks with increased postoperative pain and opioid use in orthopaedic surgery: a single-centre retrospective cohort study. British Journal of Anaesthesia, 135(5), 1286–1296. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.bja.2025.05.030

⚖️Legislation & Scope Tracker

Florida's Autonomous-Practice Bill for CRNAs Dies in Senate Rules

HB 375, which would have let CRNAs register as autonomous advanced practice registered nurses and administer anesthesia without physician oversight, stalled in the Florida Senate Rules Committee on March 13 after clearing a House committee earlier in the session. The bill would have preserved a carve-out letting individual hospitals impose their own supervision requirements even without a state mandate. Sponsors haven't announced plans to refile for 2027, but Florida's CRNA associations have signaled the fight isn't over — the state remains one of the more restrictive holdouts on the national scope-of-practice map.

Florida Senate · House Bill 375 (2026)

Ohio's Modernized CRNA Practice Law Takes Effect

Ohio House Bill 52, signed by Governor Mike DeWine and effective June 8, replaces decades-old statutory language governing CRNA practice with terms that reflect current training and care models — a state-law companion to Ohio's earlier notice to CMS that it no longer needs the federal physician-supervision opt-out requirement (our July 20 issue). Together, the two moves formalize Ohio's shift toward independent CRNA practice at both the state and federal-billing level.

💡Knowledge Pearl

Etomidate's trade-off: hemodynamic stability at the cost of the adrenal axis. Etomidate potentiates GABA-A receptor chloride currents like other induction agents, producing rapid hypnosis with minimal effect on myocardial contractility, systemic vascular resistance, or heart rate — the reason it remains a go-to induction agent for hemodynamically unstable, cardiac, and trauma patients even as broader anesthetic choices narrow this year (see this week's Drug Shortage Watch). That same stability comes with a well-known cost: etomidate reversibly inhibits 11β-hydroxylase, the enzyme that converts 11-deoxycortisol to cortisol in the adrenal cortex, suppressing endogenous cortisol synthesis for roughly 6–8 hours after even a single induction dose.

The suppression is usually clinically silent after a single bolus in an otherwise healthy patient, but it's why etomidate infusions fell out of favor for ICU sedation and why critically ill or septic patients who receive even one induction dose are sometimes considered for stress-dose steroid coverage. Etomidate's other signature quirks — myoclonus on induction (not seizure activity, though it can look like one), a comparatively high incidence of postoperative nausea and vomiting, and pain on injection — are all worth anticipating regardless of the adrenal question.

🕰️This Week in Anesthesia History
August 10, 1898

In Greifswald, Germany, surgeon August Bier administered what is generally credited as the first spinal anesthetic given specifically to enable surgery: a solution of cocaine injected into the subarachnoid space of his assistant, August Hildebrandt, after an earlier attempt on a patient had failed when too much of the drug leaked from a loose-fitting syringe. Bier and Hildebrandt then tested the block on each other — reportedly checking pain tolerance with pinpricks, cigar burns, and blows to the shin — in a demonstration that reads more like a dare than a clinical trial by modern standards. Both men suffered severe post-dural-puncture headaches for days afterward, an early and unintentional demonstration of the complication that would occupy anesthesia researchers for the next century. Bier published his findings later that year, and spinal anesthesia entered clinical practice within a few years, predating the epidural and most modern local anesthetics by decades.

🧠Board Question of the Week

A 22-year-old man undergoing elective knee arthroscopy under general anesthesia with sevoflurane develops masseter muscle rigidity, sinus tachycardia to 150 bpm, and a rising end-tidal CO2 forty minutes into the case. Over the next fifteen minutes his core temperature climbs to 39.8°C. Which intervention should be given first, and why?

Correct answer: B — Dantrolene is the specific antidote, and it can't wait

This presentation — masseter rigidity, tachycardia, rising CO2, and rapidly climbing temperature during a volatile agent — is malignant hyperthermia until proven otherwise. MH is caused by a defect, most commonly in the RYR1 gene, that leaves the ryanodine receptor abnormally sensitive to triggering agents (all volatile anesthetics and succinylcholine), producing uncontrolled calcium release from the sarcoplasmic reticulum and a hypermetabolic cascade. Dantrolene works directly at that defect, inhibiting calcium release through RYR1, and should be pushed immediately at 2.5 mg/kg, repeated up to 10 mg/kg or more until signs resolve — alongside discontinuing all triggering agents, hyperventilating with 100% oxygen at high flow, and starting active cooling.

A is dangerous rather than merely wrong: succinylcholine is itself an MH trigger and would worsen the crisis. C treats a downstream consequence — hyperkalemia — rather than the underlying calcium dysregulation, and isn't first-line; standard hyperkalemia measures are adjuncts once dantrolene is underway, not substitutes for it. D does nothing to interrupt the hypermetabolic process driving the tachycardia and would delay definitive treatment.
