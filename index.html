📰Headlines

After the Abstracts, a Named Lead Author: FIU's Peter Hsin Puts a Face on the Ketamine/Postpartum-Mood Finding

Last week's preview of ANESTHESIOLOGY 2026 flagged a meta-analysis linking perioperative ketamine or esketamine during or right after cesarean delivery to lower depressive-symptom scores in the first two weeks postpartum. Post-meeting coverage now attaches a name to that work: Peter Hsin, a researcher at Florida International University's medical school, presented the pooled analysis in San Diego, and lead investigator Rose Berkun, MD, says her group is designing a prospective, randomized follow-up at her university's anesthesiology department. Hsin himself cautioned reporters that the finding does not establish ketamine as a routine postpartum-depression prophylactic — the reduction is statistically significant in pooled early data, but optimal dosing, timing, and durability past two weeks remain unanswered, and a meta-analysis of heterogeneous trials is not the same evidentiary tier as a dedicated randomized trial. Older literature on this question is mixed: a 2024 trial of 330 patients found no difference in postpartum-depression prevalence at 3 days or 6 weeks, while a 2022 meta-analysis found benefit only in the first week. File this under promising-but-unsettled until Berkun's prospective trial reports.

CMS's Final 2027 Fee Schedule Is Due Any Day — Here's What's Actually at Stake for Anesthesia

The comment period on CMS's proposed CY 2027 Medicare Physician Fee Schedule closed September 14, and the agency's own precedent — last cycle's final rule landed October 31 — points to a release in the next several days. As of this writing, no final rule has posted. The proposal on the table would cut the anesthesia conversion factor to $20.4165 for Qualifying APM participants (down 0.89%) and $20.2143 for everyone else (down 1.38%), driven mainly by the scheduled expiration of a temporary 2.5% statutory payment bump enacted last year. ASA's comment letter pushed CMS to walk back a proposed 50% payment cut tied to modifier 25 and to add transparency around anesthesia-specific practice-expense and malpractice-cost adjustments; whether either ask survives into the final text is the actual story once CMS posts it. Groups budgeting for January 1 should expect the real numbers inside the next one to two weeks.

AANA's New President Names Workforce and Access as the Job's First Problem

Tracy Young, MSNA, MBA, CRNA — who co-founded Essential Anesthesia Management and has served as an active AANA member since 1999 — took over as the association's 2026–27 president this fall after serving as president-elect. In an interview laying out his priorities, Young put workforce and access to care first among the three biggest issues facing CRNAs, a framing that lines up with Medicus Healthcare's projection of a roughly 12,500-CRNA shortfall by 2033 and the finding that CRNAs already deliver an estimated 80% of anesthesia care in rural counties. Young's remarks didn't detail specific policy asks for his term, but the emphasis on access over, say, reimbursement mechanics signals where the association's public messaging is likely headed over the next year.

US Anesthesia Partners' Court-Ordered Overhaul Enters Its Final Stretch

The FTC's antitrust settlement with US Anesthesia Partners — reached in principle in April and stayed by a federal judge in the Southern District of Texas on May 26 — gave USAP 180 days to implement the relief the agency negotiated, a window that runs out in late November. The settlement terms themselves remain confidential while USAP executes them, and the company has not been required to admit wrongdoing; if it fails to complete the agreed changes, the FTC says it will go back to court and litigate the underlying allegations rather than simply extend the clock. Welsh Carson, the private-equity firm that backed USAP's buildup, settled separately in early 2025 and is now required to notify the FTC before further hospital-based physician-practice acquisitions. A separate private antitrust suit against USAP continues on its own track regardless of how the FTC deal resolves.

📚From the Literature

A Large New Meta-Analysis Quantifies How Much Semaglutide Changes What's in the Stomach — But Not Whether It Changes Aspiration Risk

This systematic review and meta-analysis pooled 12 observational studies (10 cohort, 2 case-control; no randomized trials) covering 49,651 perioperative or pre-endoscopy patients, 11,022 of them on semaglutide. The headline finding: semaglutide use was associated with residual gastric content in 14.1% of patients versus 5.2% of controls, a pooled relative risk of 3.49 (95% CI 1.78–6.83, moderate-certainty evidence) — an absolute increase of about 9 percentage points. Digestive symptoms (nausea, bloating, early satiety) were also far more common on semaglutide: 17.3% versus 3.2% (RR 5.65, 95% CI 3.61–8.84). But the outcome anesthesia providers actually care about — pulmonary aspiration — showed no statistically significant difference: 0.24% versus 0.37% (RR 1.46, 95% CI 0.29–7.24, very-low-certainty evidence), and the authors calculate that detecting a real difference in aspiration risk, if one exists, would take a study of roughly 300,000–400,000 participants. Procedure discontinuation and gastroparesis rates likewise trended higher on semaglutide without reaching significance. The takeaway for practice: the stomach-content signal is real and sizable, but it is still an indirect marker, not proof that current patients face a materially higher aspiration rate — which is exactly why professional-society guidance leans on symptom screening and point-of-care ultrasound rather than a blanket mandate.

World Journal of Gastrointestinal Surgery · Ballotin, V. R., Ferreira, P. L. C., de Almeida, V. T., Borges, C. da S., de Almeida, H. T., da Silva, R. G. P., Volquind, D., & Selistre, L. da S. (2026). Residual gastric content and aspiration-related outcomes in perioperative patients treated with semaglutide: A systematic review and meta-analysis. World Journal of Gastrointestinal Surgery, 18(9), Article 121449. https://doi.org/10.4240/wjgs.121449

Published online September 27, 2026 — well within the five-year window and the largest pooled dataset on this question to date.

⚖️Legislation & Scope Tracker

Ohio Finishes the Loop: A New State Collaboration Standard, Then a Federal Opt-Out to Match It

Ohio's House Bill 52, signed by Gov. Mike DeWine on March 10, 2026, and effective June 8, replaced the state's physician-supervision requirement for CRNAs with a collaboration model — a CRNA now practices in collaboration with a physician, podiatrist, or dentist who has requested their services, still bound by the privileges and policies of the facility where they work, rather than under direct supervision. The Ohio Society of Anesthesiologists was careful to note the law still requires a defined physician relationship and does not permit nurse-only anesthesia. A month after the state law took effect, DeWine sent CMS a letter of attestation opting Ohio out of Medicare's separate federal physician-supervision requirement entirely, making it the 27th state (plus Guam) to do so under the mechanism CMS has allowed governors to invoke since a November 2001 rule. The sequencing matters: Ohio changed its own scope law first, then used that change as the basis to drop the federal supervision requirement too — a two-step playbook other states weighing opt-out may watch closely.

AANA · Ohio Opts Out of Physician Supervision for CRNAs

The National Opt-Out Map Now Covers More Than Half the Country — With Two States Still Stuck at “Partial”

AANA's July 2026 fact sheet puts the federal Medicare opt-out count at 27 states plus Guam, following Vermont (26th, Gov. Phil Scott's letter July 2) and Ohio (27th, July 10) in quick succession. Not every state on the list is a clean yes-or-no, though: Colorado converted from a partial to a full opt-out back in October 2023, while Utah and Wyoming remain partial opt-outs years later, meaning the federal supervision requirement still applies to some subset of cases in those two states depending on setting or payer rules. For SRNAs training across state lines or weighing where to practice after graduation, the practical lesson is that “opt-out state” is not a single uniform status — always check whether a given state's opt-out is full or partial, and what the state's own scope law (not just the federal supervision waiver) separately requires.

💡Knowledge Pearl

Why a GLP-1 receptor agonist can leave a patient with a full stomach despite a textbook fasting period — and what that means at induction.

Semaglutide and other GLP-1 receptor agonists mimic the gut hormone glucagon-like peptide-1, which normally rises after eating to augment glucose-dependent insulin secretion, suppress glucagon, and — the part that matters most here — slow gastric emptying via vagally mediated effects on antral motility and pyloric tone. That delayed-emptying effect is dose-related, can persist for the drug's full dosing interval (days for daily formulations, up to a week or more for weekly ones like semaglutide), and is exactly why standard NPO guidance — built around a healthy stomach's normal emptying time — does not reliably predict residual gastric volume in a patient on these drugs. This week's literature item quantifies that gap: a nearly three-and-a-half-fold increase in detectable residual gastric content despite patients following ordinary fasting instructions.

Clinically, that's the rationale behind current society guidance to treat GLP-1 use as a full-stomach risk factor to be actively assessed rather than assumed away by the clock: screen for GI symptoms (nausea, bloating, early satiety) on the morning of the case, and when in doubt, use point-of-care gastric antral ultrasound to grade content as empty, clear-liquid, or solid/thick-fluid. A clear or empty antrum supports standard induction; a solid or indeterminate full antrum should be managed as a full stomach — rapid-sequence induction, or deferring the case — regardless of how many hours the patient reports fasting. The nuance worth remembering for boards: as this week's meta-analysis shows, that increased residual content has not yet been shown to translate into a statistically significant rise in actual aspiration events, so the guidance is a precaution built on a plausible mechanism and an indirect marker, not yet on a proven increase in bad outcomes.

🕰️This Week in Anesthesia History
November 1, 1815

This week marks the birth, on November 1, 1815, in Danielsville, Georgia, of Crawford Williamson Long — the rural physician who administered ether to remove a tumor from medical student James Venable's neck on March 30, 1842, four years before William Morton's famous public demonstration at Massachusetts General Hospital. Long simply didn't publish. Preoccupied with a growing country practice and, by some accounts, uncertain his observation would be believed without more cases behind it, he waited until 1849 to put his results in print, by which time Morton's Ether Dome demonstration had already claimed the public credit and the headlines. Jackson himself later conceded Long's priority in actually using ether for surgery, and the United States now marks National Doctors' Day each March 30 in honor of that first operation rather than Morton's better-known one in October. It's a useful corrective to pair with last week's Ether Dome anniversary: the first public demonstration and the first actual use of surgical anesthesia were performed by two different men, four years apart, and only one of them got famous for it.

🧠Board Question of the Week

A 52-year-old patient taking weekly semaglutide for type 2 diabetes presents for an elective procedure under general anesthesia. The patient followed standard fasting guidelines (NPO for solids ≥8 hours, clear liquids ≥2 hours) but reports feeling unusually full and mildly nauseated this morning. Which of the following is the most appropriate next step before induction?

Correct answer: B — assess before assuming the fast was adequate

GLP-1 receptor agonists like semaglutide slow gastric emptying through vagally mediated effects on antral motility, an effect that can outlast standard NPO intervals and is not reliably predicted by fasting time alone. Current guidance calls for screening this patient population for GI symptoms and, especially when symptoms are present, using point-of-care gastric ultrasound to grade stomach content before deciding on an induction plan — treating a positive full-stomach finding as grounds for rapid-sequence induction or deferral, rather than routine induction.

A ignores the documented symptoms and the known pharmacology that makes standard fasting guidance unreliable in this population. C treats the nausea as the problem to be medicated away rather than a sign to investigate aspiration risk, and still skips assessment. D overcorrects: the evidence base (including this week's literature item) shows GLP-1 use raises the rate of residual gastric content, not a confirmed rise in aspiration events, so a blanket automatic cancellation without any individualized assessment is not what current guidance recommends.
