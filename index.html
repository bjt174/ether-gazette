📰Headlines

ANESTHESIOLOGY 2026 Wraps Up in San Diego With Previews on Ketamine, Ibuprofen, and Cannabis

The American Society of Anesthesiologists' ANESTHESIOLOGY 2026 annual meeting ran October 16–20 at the San Diego Convention Center, drawing anesthesiologists, CRNAs, and trainees for more than 1,000 scientific abstracts under the hashtag #ANES26. Research previewed ahead of the meeting pointed in several directions worth a second look: one study found that ketamine given during cesarean delivery may ease early postpartum-depression symptoms; another suggested ibuprofen and other NSAIDs may lower delirium risk after surgery; a third linked cannabis use to a higher risk of perioperative heart attack but a lower risk of postsurgical blood clots; and a fourth found that patients who took a sleep aid before surgery slept better, hurt less, and used fewer opioids afterward. None of these are published peer-reviewed papers yet — they're meeting abstracts, which carry less evidentiary weight than a completed trial until the data clears peer review — so treat the headlines as hypotheses to watch rather than practice changes.

CMS's Proposed 2027 Pay Cut Heads Toward a Final Rule as ASA Keeps Pushing Back

CMS's proposed CY 2027 Medicare Physician Fee Schedule would trim both anesthesia conversion factors — the Qualifying APM rate to $20.4165 (down 0.89%) and the non-Qualifying rate to $20.2143 (down 1.38%) — driven mainly by the scheduled expiration of a temporary 2.5% statutory payment bump and a budget-neutrality adjustment tied to other coding changes. The ASA has pushed CMS to roll back several adjacent provisions, including a proposed 50% payment cut for services billed with modifier 25 and what it calls insufficient transparency around anesthesia-specific practice-expense and malpractice adjustments. The comment period closed September 14, and CMS typically issues the final rule on or around November 1 for a January 1 effective date — meaning the real numbers, and whether any of ASA's asks land, should be known within the next two weeks.

A New Staffing Report Puts Numbers to the Anesthesia Workforce Crunch: Thousands Short, and Getting Worse

A new report from Medicus Healthcare Solutions, “The State of Anesthesia Care Delivery,” projects a 2026 national anesthesiologist shortfall of roughly 3,720 full-time-equivalent positions, more than doubling to about 8,140 by 2031, with the gap landing hardest outside metro areas — non-metro markets face a projected shortage of 6,260 FTEs even as metro markets show a surplus. On the CRNA side, other Medicus projections put the shortfall at roughly 12,500 by 2033, nearly 22% of today's workforce. The pipeline isn't closing the gap quickly: about 44% of U.S. medical students seeking an anesthesiology residency didn't match in 2024, nurse-anesthesia programs accept roughly a quarter of applicants, and nearly a third of the current CRNA workforce is 55 or older. Retention is shaky too — 42% of anesthesiologists say they're likely to leave their current role within two years — and more than 80% of rural counties have no anesthesiologist at all, leaving CRNAs, who already deliver an estimated 80% of rural anesthesia care, to fill much of that gap.

U.S. Drug Shortages Hit a Third Straight Quarterly High, and Anesthesia Drugs Are Among the Hardest Hit

Active U.S. drug shortages climbed to 227 in the second quarter of 2026 — the third consecutive quarterly increase — according to tracking from the American Society of Health-System Pharmacists and the University of Utah. That's still well below the record 323 shortages logged in early 2024, but the trend line is headed the wrong way. Nearly half of this year's new shortages trace to a single manufacturer, and 16% involve controlled substances. 📚From the Literature

A Fresh Meta-Analysis Finds a Modest, Shaky Signal for Esketamine and Postpartum Mood After Cesarean

This systematic review and meta-analysis pooled randomized controlled trials testing perioperative esketamine during cesarean delivery, with ten trials feeding the main pain-and-recovery analysis and five of those also reporting postpartum mood outcomes. The postpartum-depression-adjacent finding: pooled Edinburgh Postnatal Depression Scale scores were lower in the esketamine group than controls (standardized mean difference −0.21, 95% CI −0.39 to −0.04, p = 0.02), which is the same direction as the ketamine/postpartum-depression finding previewed for this week's ASA meeting above. But the authors flag the result as statistically fragile — a leave-one-out sensitivity analysis showed the estimate shifted substantially depending on which single trial was dropped, so they treat it as exploratory rather than confirmed. A secondary finding worth knowing clinically: women over 30 showed meaningfully higher dizziness risk with esketamine in a subgroup analysis, roughly double that of controls. The biggest caveat for a U.S.-trained SRNA: all ten included trials were conducted in China, where esketamine is approved for this indication, so the authors themselves caution the findings may not generalize beyond that population.

Frontiers in Pharmacology · Jiang, T., & Zhao, R. (2025). Efficacy and safety of esketamine for pain control after cesarean delivery: A systematic review and meta-analysis of randomized controlled trials. Frontiers in Pharmacology, 16, Article 1708221. https://doi.org/10.3389/fphar.2025.1708221

Published December 1, 2025 — well within the five-year window and one of the most recent analyses on this question.

⚖️Legislation & Scope Tracker

Rhode Island's H7426 Would Let Experienced CRNAs Practice Without Physician Supervision

A bill introduced in Rhode Island's House this session, H7426, would allow CRNAs with at least two years of post-certification experience to practice without physician supervision, while the same bill still defines a separate “supervised” category for newer CRNAs. It's running alongside Senate action: a related bill, S3184, drew committee testimony this spring, including an April 30 letter from a CRNA arguing that elective deep sedation should stay limited to anesthesia-trained professionals — a preview of where organized pushback will likely concentrate if either bill advances. As written, H7426 would take effect January 1, 2027, but as of this writing it remains in committee with no floor vote scheduled, so Rhode Island joins the long list of states where a scope expansion is drafted but not yet decided.

A Scope-and-Pay Roundup: South Carolina, Louisiana, and New York Each Have a CRNA Bill Pending

Three other states have their own CRNA-related bills moving at different speeds this session. South Carolina's identical bills S.360 and H.4044 would repeal the state's physician/dentist supervision requirement for CRNAs outright and add prescriptive authority — introduced in both chambers, evidently as a belt-and-suspenders move, and still pending in the 2025–2026 session that runs into next spring. Louisiana took the narrower payment-parity route instead with SB 275, now Act 257: it requires insurers to reimburse CRNAs practicing without physician or dentist direction at the same rate as physicians, explicitly without changing anyone's scope of practice, effective January 1, 2027. And New York's S357-B/A6771-A would do something more basic than either — write a CRNA title and scope of practice into state law for the first time, which supporters say the state currently lacks entirely. Worth tracking together: this cycle's pattern looks less like new physician-supervision opt-outs and more like states filling in legal gaps that the opt-out wave left behind.

💡Knowledge Pearl

Why ketamine's mechanism makes this week's postpartum-mood headlines biologically plausible — and why it behaves so differently from the IV anesthetics around it.

Ketamine's core action is non-competitive antagonism of the NMDA glutamate receptor, not the GABA-A potentiation that drives propofol, etomidate, and the volatiles. Blocking NMDA receptors disrupts normal thalamocortical signaling while leaving limbic and other subcortical circuits comparatively active — the basis of the “dissociative” state, in which a patient can appear to have open eyes and preserved airway reflexes while being functionally unaware. That same NMDA blockade is also the mechanistic link behind this week's ketamine/esketamine-and-mood findings: NMDA antagonism is the proposed basis for ketamine's rapid-acting antidepressant effect in treatment-resistant depression more broadly, which is why a perioperative dose during cesarean delivery is a biologically reasonable thing to test for postpartum mood — even though, as this week's literature item shows, the clinical evidence for that specific use is still thin and unstable.

Clinically, the same mechanism explains ketamine's unusual safety profile in a few specific populations: because it typically preserves or increases sympathetic tone (via inhibition of catecholamine reuptake) rather than blunting it, ketamine tends to support blood pressure and heart rate in hypovolemic or septic patients where propofol or etomidate might not, and its bronchodilating effect via circulating catecholamines makes it a reasonable induction choice in active bronchospasm. The tradeoff is the same NMDA blockade producing emergence delirium, hallucinations, and nystagmus on the way out — which is why a benzodiazepine is still commonly paired with it despite not being strictly required for the anesthetic effect itself.

🕰️This Week in Anesthesia History
October 16, 1846

On the morning of October 16, 1846, dentist William T. G. Morton administered sulfuric ether to patient Edward Gilbert Abbott in the surgical amphitheater at Massachusetts General Hospital — the room now preserved as the “Ether Dome” — while surgeon John Collins Warren excised a vascular tumor from Abbott's jaw and neck. Warren's reported remark to the assembled students afterward, “Gentlemen, this is no humbug,” marked the moment public surgery stopped being synonymous with screaming patients, brute restraint, and amputations measured in seconds. Word spread fast: Dr. Henry Jacob Bigelow's account in the Boston Medical and Surgical Journal, combined with competing claims to credit from Morton, dentist Horace Wells, and chemist Charles Jackson, carried news of the demonstration across the Atlantic within weeks, and ether anesthesia was in documented use on multiple continents within the year. This October marks the 180th anniversary of that single morning — and, fittingly, it fell during the same week the American Society of Anesthesiologists held its 2026 annual meeting a few miles south along the same coast, presenting research on everything from ketamine's NMDA pharmacology to postoperative delirium that Morton's audience could never have imagined asking about.

🧠Board Question of the Week

During an ultrasound-guided interscalene brachial plexus block with 30 mL of 0.5% bupivacaine for shoulder arthroscopy, the patient becomes agitated, reports circumoral numbness and tinnitus, and then rapidly develops seizure activity. Which of the following is the most appropriate immediate step?

Correct answer: B — this is local anesthetic systemic toxicity (LAST)

Circumoral numbness, tinnitus, and a metallic taste are classic early-warning signs of LAST from systemic local anesthetic absorption during a vascular-rich block like an interscalene approach; the presentation can progress within minutes to seizures and, with bupivacaine specifically, life-threatening cardiac dysrhythmias or arrest, because bupivacaine's high protein binding and strong cardiac sodium-channel affinity make it disproportionately cardiotoxic once systemic levels rise.

ASRA's LAST checklist calls for immediately stopping the injection, calling for help, managing the airway with 100% oxygen to prevent the hypoxia and acidosis that worsen cardiotoxicity, controlling seizures with a benzodiazepine, and starting 20% lipid emulsion early (roughly a 1.5 mL/kg lean-body-weight bolus followed by an infusion) to help sequester the lipophilic local anesthetic.

A dismisses a true emergency as self-limited and withholds specific treatment. C misattributes a LAST presentation to an opioid reaction and proposes resuming injection into a patient who is actively toxic. D is actively dangerous — adding more local anesthetic during an evolving LAST event is the opposite of indicated care.
