📰Headlines

CMS's Final 2027 Rule Lands a Deeper Anesthesia Cut Than Its Own July Proposal

The CY 2027 Medicare Physician Fee Schedule final rule, released November 4 and now working its way through practice finance teams, confirms what last issue's dispatch couldn't yet pin down: the anesthesia-specific conversion factor is falling further than CMS's own July proposal suggested. The qualifying-APM anesthesia rate lands at $20.3068, versus a $20.4165 proposal, while the non-qualifying rate drops to $20.1056 — a 1.91% cut from 2026's $20.4976, compared with the gentler 1.38% reduction CMS had floated over the summer. Anesthesia base units are unchanged for 2027, so the cut flows entirely through the conversion factor rather than the units themselves. ASA's September comment letter had pushed CMS to soften both numbers; the final rule suggests that effort bought little ground, leaving group practices and ASC anesthesia programs to rework 2027 budgets around a steeper reduction than they'd planned for.

Anesthesia Stipends Go Mainstream at ASCs Even as Provider Access Eases

Two trend lines that look contradictory are actually running together: the ASC Association's third-quarter 2026 survey found the share of surgery centers reporting adequate anesthesia-provider access at its highest level since 2022, even as stipend-based coverage keeps spreading. NorthStar Anesthesia reported adding 17 new ASC partnerships nationwide so far in 2026, and its CEO said roughly 80% of the company's ASC network now runs on anesthesia stipends rather than straight fee-for-service. The same survey found nearly a third of ASCs now build financial assistance into their anesthesia contracts. Read together, the picture is a market that has stabilized coverage largely by agreeing to pay more for it directly — a dynamic that sits uneasily next to this week's CMS fee cut above.

Becker's ASC · Anesthesia Crunch Eases for ASCs; Big Centers Still Squeezed

Anesthesiologist Pay Climbs as Much as 16%, Accelerating Group Consolidation

SullivanCotter's 2026 Physician Compensation and Productivity Survey, released September 17, found total cash compensation for general, cardiac, and pediatric anesthesiologists up 14–16% since 2024 — among the fastest pay growth of any specialty the survey tracks. SullivanCotter's own analysis ties that growth directly to this year's wave of private-practice consolidation, arguing that the pay run-up has "markedly shifted employment models" as smaller groups weigh selling or merging rather than competing for staff against better-funded employers. The figure complicates the usual shortage narrative: pay is rising fast even as practices describe a tightening labor market, suggesting the squeeze is showing up in deal-making as much as in open positions.

📚From the Literature

A New North American Guideline Asks Three Questions to Decide Who Needs Malignant Hyperthermia Genetic Testing

A multidisciplinary expert panel led by Sheila Riazi proposes a simple triage: has the patient had a malignant hyperthermia (MH) reaction or known susceptibility, has a relative had one, and has anyone in the family already had genetic testing? A "yes" to any of those routes the patient toward genetic testing of RYR1, CACNA1S, and STAC3 — now considered first-line — rather than relying solely on the older, more invasive caffeine-halothane contracture test. The plain-language catch for practice: genetic testing's sensitivity tops out around 70%, so a negative result narrows risk but doesn't clear a patient outright, and clinical vigilance around triggering agents still applies regardless of genotype. The guideline was published jointly in Anesthesiology and the Canadian Journal of Anesthesia, reflecting its intended use on both sides of the border.

⚖️Legislation & Scope Tracker

A Rural Anesthesiologist Bill Clears Committee 41–0, Then Stalls; Rhode Island's Expansion Bill Appears Dead

H.R. 9642, the Medicare Access to Rural Anesthesiology Act, would let rural and critical-access hospitals tap the same Medicare Part A funds they currently use only to hire CRNAs or anesthesiologist assistants to instead employ or contract a physician anesthesiologist — aimed at the roughly 81% of rural counties sponsors say lack one. The House Ways and Means Committee approved it unanimously back on July 15, and the Congressional Budget Office reissued its cost estimate (a $137 million Part A increase over 2027–2036, offset partly by lower Part B spending) on October 9 — but no House floor vote has materialized since, leaving the bill in a holding pattern despite the committee's unanimity. Separately, Rhode Island's H7426, which would have dropped the physician-supervision requirement for CRNAs with two or more years of experience, now shows as dead in legislative tracking, while Florida's HB 375 — full practice authority for registered autonomous CRNAs, passed by the House 78–28 in February — has shown no recorded Senate action since being referred to Rules in early February and its fate for this session is unclear. The 27-state Medicare opt-out count noted in prior issues is unchanged this week.

💡Knowledge Pearl

Etomidate's single biggest clinical liability traces to one enzyme it blocks almost by accident. Etomidate is prized for its hemodynamic stability on induction because it has minimal effect on sympathetic tone or myocardial contractility — but that same imidazole ring that makes it so cardiostable also inhibits 11β-hydroxylase, the mitochondrial enzyme that converts 11-deoxycortisol to cortisol (and, upstream, supports aldosterone synthesis) in the adrenal cortex. A single induction dose measurably suppresses cortisol production for roughly 12–24 hours in most patients — clinically silent in a healthy patient having a short case, but a real concern in septic or critically ill patients who depend on an intact stress response.

That mechanism is why etomidate has fallen out of favor for sustained infusions (now avoided entirely for that reason) and why its use in septic shock remains debated — the single-dose adrenal suppression is real and reproducible, but large trials haven't consistently tied a single induction dose to worse clinical outcomes. Board questions often pair this fact with etomidate's other classic trade-offs: no analgesic properties, a high rate of myoclonus and pain on injection, and preserved cerebral perfusion pressure that makes it a frequent choice for induction in hemodynamically unstable or neurotrauma patients despite the adrenal issue.

November 21, 1846
🕰️This Week in Anesthesia History

Six weeks after Morton's public ether demonstration, physician-poet Oliver Wendell Holmes Sr. sat down and wrote his friend a letter about what to call the new state the gas produced. Morton and his backers had been marketing the vapor under the deliberately vague trade name "Letheon," trying to protect a patent on what was, chemically, just sulfuric ether — a strategy the medical establishment viewed with open suspicion. Holmes's letter, dated November 21, 1846, proposed cleaner, more honest terminology: "anaesthesia" for the state of insensibility, drawn from the Greek an- ("without") and aisthesis ("sensation"), and "anaesthetic" for the agent producing it. The terms stuck almost immediately in the medical literature, outliving the patent fight, the marketing name, and most of the men involved in the original dispute — a rare case of a brand-new field getting its name right on the first try.

🧠Board Question of the Week

A 19-year-old patient undergoing elective tonsillectomy receives succinylcholine for intubation. Fifteen minutes into the case, end-tidal CO2 rises sharply, heart rate increases to 140 bpm, and the patient's temperature climbs from 36.8°C to 38.9°C over the next ten minutes. Masseter rigidity was noted on intubation. Which of the following is the most appropriate immediate treatment, in addition to stopping all triggering agents and hyperventilating with 100% oxygen?

Correct answer: B — IV dantrolene

This is malignant hyperthermia (MH): a hypermetabolic crisis triggered by a volatile anesthetic or succinylcholine in a genetically susceptible patient (classically an RYR1 variant), presenting with rising end-tidal CO2, tachycardia, masseter rigidity, and rapidly climbing temperature. Dantrolene is the only specific treatment — it works by blocking calcium release through the ryanodine receptor on the sarcoplasmic reticulum, directly counteracting the uncontrolled calcium efflux driving the hypermetabolic state. It should be given immediately at the first strong suspicion of MH, not held in reserve, at 2.5 mg/kg IV, repeated every 5–10 minutes as needed up to 10 mg/kg.

Cooling (A) is an important adjunct but doesn't address the underlying calcium dysregulation and should never substitute for or delay dantrolene. Calcium chloride (C) has a role later if hyperkalemia causes arrhythmia, but it's not the immediate priority and doesn't treat the root cause. Additional neuromuscular blockade (D) — especially a nondepolarizer, which wouldn't touch MH-related rigidity anyway — does nothing for the hypermetabolic process itself and wastes critical time that should go toward dantrolene administration and cooling.
