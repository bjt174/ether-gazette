📰Headlines

A Small French Trial Suggests Scheduled Naps Could Sharpen Residents' Performance on 24-Hour Shifts

A randomized study of 27 anesthesiology residents, published in the October 2026 issue of Anesthesiology, trained participants in a short nap protocol, then randomized them during 24-hour call shifts to either a monitored 30-minute nap opportunity or quiet wakeful rest without sleep. Afterward, each resident ran a simulated cardiac-arrest crisis on a training manikin. Residents who napped scored about 7% higher on overall crisis-management performance than those who didn't, with technical-skill scores trending better but not reaching statistical significance. The authors, led by Laura Schmidt of Université Claude Bernard Lyon 1, are careful to frame this as initial evidence rather than proof: the sample is small, the outcome was a simulation rather than real patient care, and nap logistics on a busy call night remain an open question. Still, it's a data point in favor of protected nap time as a fatigue-mitigation strategy rather than an afterthought.

The FTC's Settlement With U.S. Anesthesia Partners Enters Its Final Stretch

The FTC's antitrust case against U.S. Anesthesia Partners (USAP) over its Texas practice roll-up has been in an implementation lull since a federal judge stayed the litigation on May 26 so the parties could finalize a settlement reached in principle on April 23. USAP has up to 180 days from the stay — a window running toward late November — to carry out the agreed relief before any final, court-approved order locks it in; if USAP doesn't fully execute, the FTC says it will go back to court and litigate the underlying acquisitions. Separately, the FTC's related consent order against USAP's former private-equity backer, Welsh Carson Anderson & Stowe, has already been final since May 2025 and requires the firm to notify the FTC before making future acquisitions or new board appointments in anesthesia and other hospital-based physician practices. A private follow-on suit, Burbage v. USAP, continues on a separate track toward class certification, with a trial currently set for January 2028 — a reminder that even a finished FTC settlement won't close the book on USAP's legal exposure.

An Open Class I Recall on Dräger's Vapor 2000 and 3000 Vaporizers Is Still Working Its Way Through U.S. ORs

Dräger's November 2025 recall of certain Vapor 2000 and Vapor 3000 unheated vaporizer units remains open and classified nearly a year later, per the FDA's recall database (Z-0934-2026). The issue traces to a supplier component contaminated with metal-soldering residue that can react with volatile fluorinated anesthetic agents, with a theoretical risk of inhaled or ingested impurities causing tracheobronchial irritation, lung tissue damage, or pulmonary edema; Dräger had reported no serious injuries or deaths as of December 1, 2025. Affected serial numbers are being replaced by local Dräger representatives, and facilities still running an affected unit are instructed to follow the device's cleaning instructions and use inspiratory filters in the interim. Worth a check of your site's vaporizer inventory against Dräger's published serial-number list if that hasn't already happened.

📚From the Literature

The GA-CARES Trial Finds No Survival or Recurrence Benefit From Propofol Over Volatile Anesthesia in Cancer Surgery

This pragmatic, multicenter randomized trial assigned 1,763 patients undergoing resection of aggressive cancers at five U.S. centers to propofol- or volatile-agent-based anesthesia maintenance and followed them for at least two years. In the primary intention-to-treat analysis, all-cause mortality didn't differ significantly between groups (26.1% propofol vs. 22.9% volatile; hazard ratio 1.16, 95% CI 0.96–1.41; p = 0.115). A secondary per-protocol analysis — patients who actually received their assigned anesthetic throughout — found significantly more deaths in the propofol group (25.5% vs. 20%; hazard ratio 1.31, 95% CI 1.05–1.64; p = 0.017), though the authors flag this result as statistically fragile given its secondary, non-randomized-comparison nature. Disease-free survival, a secondary endpoint in patients having curative-intent surgery, showed no meaningful difference either (hazard ratio 1.10, 95% CI 0.9–1.36). For SRNAs, the bottom line is that this trial doesn't support the lab-bench hypothesis that propofol's immunomodulatory profile translates into better cancer outcomes than a volatile agent — so agent choice in oncologic surgery can keep resting on hemodynamic goals, PONV risk, and cost rather than a presumed oncologic edge.

Anesthesiology · Bennett-Guerrero, E., Romeiser, J. L., DeMaria, S., Nadler, J. W., Quinn, T. D., Ponnappan, S. K., Wang, R., Gloff, M. S., Lee, K. J., Levin, M. A., Sroka, R. D., Stanley, S. L., Wang, D., Sasson, A. R., & the GA-CARES Trial Group. (2026). Anesthesia type during cancer surgery: Results of the GA-CARES randomized, multicenter trial. ⚖️Legislation & Scope Tracker

Federal "Anesthesia for All Act" Returns, Still Stuck in Committee

Rep. Ritchie Torres (D-NY) reintroduced the Anesthesia for All Act (H.R. 6545) on December 9, 2025, a year after its original version stalled. The bill would bar health plans — including Medicaid, group health plans, and private insurers — from reducing anesthesia payment solely because a case ran past a predetermined time limit, require payment to follow what the treating anesthesiologist or licensed anesthesia provider judges medically necessary, and direct HHS's Office of Inspector General to audit payer compliance. The push traces back to Anthem Blue Cross Blue Shield's 2024 proposal to cap anesthesia reimbursement by surgical time limit in Connecticut, New York, and Missouri — a policy Anthem withdrew under pressure but which has since spawned a wave of state-level bans. As of this writing, the bill has one cosponsor (Del. Eleanor Holmes Norton) and sits with the House Energy and Commerce Committee, with no scheduled markup.

Washington's Anesthesia Time-Cap Ban Would Give Regulators Real Teeth

House Bill 1812, reintroduced in Washington's legislature on January 29, 2026, would bar insurers from denying coverage or reducing reimbursement for anesthesia services based on a predetermined time limit — joining Missouri, Maryland, and Illinois in the now-familiar post-Anthem wave of state bans. What sets Washington's version apart is enforcement: the bill would hand the Office of the Insurance Commissioner explicit authority to levy monetary penalties, order repayment of improperly denied claims, and pursue license suspension or revocation against noncompliant insurers, rather than leaving the ban to be enforced solely through private litigation. The bill remains in committee as of this writing; its prior 2025 version did not reach a floor vote.

💡Knowledge Pearl

Why succinylcholine can trigger life-threatening hyperkalemia in burn, denervation, and prolonged-immobility patients — but not in everyone. Under normal conditions, nicotinic acetylcholine receptors on skeletal muscle sit concentrated at the neuromuscular junction, and a typical dose of succinylcholine depolarizes only that focal area, releasing a clinically trivial amount of potassium into the circulation. But burns, denervation injuries, severe infection, and prolonged immobility all trigger upregulation of extrajunctional nicotinic receptors across the entire muscle membrane — a compensatory response to disrupted neural signaling that starts within days and can persist for months after the inciting injury resolves.

When succinylcholine depolarizes a membrane studded with these extrajunctional receptors, the potassium efflux is no longer confined to the junction — it happens across the whole cell surface, and the resulting hyperkalemia can be massive enough to cause cardiac arrest within minutes of induction. This is why succinylcholine is generally avoided starting around 24–48 hours after a major burn or denervating injury (the exact window is debated, but risk is thought to persist for 6 months to a year), and it's a direct, mechanistic reason to reach for a nondepolarizing agent like rocuronium instead when a patient's history includes recent burns, spinal cord injury, stroke, or prolonged critical illness — a decision that matters even more this week given rocuronium's own spot on the active-shortage list above.

🕰️This Week in Anesthesia History
October 17, 1939

On October 17, 1939, the National Association of Nurse Anesthetists — founded eight years earlier in 1931 under Agatha Hodgins — renamed itself and reincorporated in Illinois as the American Association of Nurse Anesthetists. The move came as the young profession was still establishing its institutional footing: a formal certification examination wouldn't arrive until 1945, and accreditation standards for training programs were still years off. The organization would go through one more identity change nearly eight decades later, rebranding as the American Association of Nurse Anesthesiology in 2017 to reflect the title change from "nurse anesthetist" to "nurse anesthesiologist" that some states now recognize. Agatha Hodgins herself never saw either rename — she died in 1945, the same year the organization's first certification exam was administered — but the Illinois reincorporation she set in motion in 1939 is the direct legal ancestor of the AANA that represents more than 50,000 CRNAs and SRNAs today.

🧠Board Question of the Week

Ninety minutes into a monopolar transurethral resection of the prostate (TURP) performed under spinal anesthesia, with the patient sedated and breathing spontaneously, the patient becomes acutely confused and nauseated, then develops hypertension with reflex bradycardia and reports visual disturbances, describing everything as tinted green. Which of the following is the most appropriate immediate next step?

Correct answer: B — this presentation is classic TURP syndrome from irrigation-fluid absorption

Monopolar TURP requires a non-ionic, electrolyte-free irrigation fluid (commonly glycine 1.5% or a sorbitol-mannitol mix) because ionic fluids would disperse the electrocautery current. That fluid is absorbed systemically through the venous sinuses opened by resection, producing dilutional hyponatremia and relative hypo-osmolality that can cause cerebral edema. The resulting picture — confusion, nausea, hypertension with reflex bradycardia (a Cushing-like response to rising intracranial pressure), and the pathognomonic transient visual disturbance from glycine's action on the retina — is textbook TURP syndrome. Immediate management is to have the surgeon finish or halt the resection to stop further fluid absorption, obtain a stat sodium, and correct symptomatic hyponatremia with hypertonic saline when indicated, correcting gradually to avoid osmotic demyelination syndrome.

A dismisses a specific, mechanistically explainable warning sign as benign. C treats agitation without addressing the underlying cerebral edema and risks worsening sedation-related hypoventilation. D treats a presumed volume-overload problem that is actually a dilutional, hypo-osmolar electrolyte emergency — furosemide does nothing to correct the serum sodium and delays definitive treatment.
