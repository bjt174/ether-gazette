📰Headlines

CMS Finalizes the CY 2027 Fee Schedule, Ending Four Months of Waiting — But Not the Anesthesia Suspense

The cliffhanger from last week's dispatch has a resolution of sorts: CMS issued the final CY 2027 Medicare Physician Fee Schedule on November 4 and scheduled it for publication in the Federal Register today, November 9. The headline numbers are now locked in — a general conversion factor of $32.9548 for qualifying APM participants (down 1.83%) and $32.6286 for everyone else (down 2.31%) — along with a finalized 20% payment reduction for the lower-valued service when modifier 25 is billed alongside a same-day, zero-day-global procedure. What the initial rollout has not yet made clear is the anesthesia-specific conversion factor that actually drives OR reimbursement; CMS had proposed $20.4165 for qualifying APM participants and $20.2143 for everyone else back in July, and ASA's September comment letter pushed to soften both. Whether the finalized anesthesia numbers track the general cut or land somewhere ASA can call a partial win is the detail group practices and ASC anesthesia programs are now digging through the rule text to find before January 1.

Advertised CRNA Pay Crosses $300,000, Sharpening the Anesthesiologist-vs-CRNA Pay Debate

Median advertised CRNA pay hit $300,000 in September 2026, up roughly 7% from $280,000 a year earlier, according to a job-posting index compiled by Anesthesia Operations Consultants — separate from a Bureau of Labor Statistics figure that puts average CRNA salary at $276,434 as of April. The number lands against a backdrop Becker's has been tracking all year: Medicare pays the same fee-schedule amount for a solo CRNA case as for a solo anesthesiologist case, and splits payment 50/50 when an anesthesiologist medically directs a CRNA. With CMS's 2027 rate cut now finalized and ASC leaders increasingly leaning on stipend-based coverage models rather than straight fee-for-service, the pay gap between the two tracks — and who absorbs the squeeze — is shaping up as one of 2027's bigger workforce storylines.

📚From the Literature

Standard-Dose Naloxone Struggles to Fully Reverse Fentanyl and Sufentanil Overdoses

A prospective crossover trial in 12 opioid-naive volunteers and 18 daily opioid users found that intranasal naloxone (4 mg) restored minute ventilation within minutes across the board, but full recovery of end-tidal CO2 lagged by 11–17 minutes — and for sufentanil, a higher-affinity mu-opioid agonist, full CO2 recovery never occurred in several participants within the study window. The plain-language takeaway for practice: a single standard naloxone dose may restore breathing effort without fully reversing the underlying respiratory depression, particularly with potent synthetic opioids — which is exactly why ASA's commentary on the study urges bystanders and clinicians to call 911 and be ready to redose rather than treat one naloxone administration as the end of the emergency.

⚖️Legislation & Scope Tracker

The Opt-Out Count Holds at 27 States, While Rhode Island Weighs Going Further

Ohio's July 10 letter to CMS made it the 27th state to opt out of the federal Medicare physician-supervision requirement for CRNAs, following Vermont's opt-out on July 2. Ohio's move rode in on House Bill 52, which replaced the state's old CRNA "supervision" standard with a collaboration model involving a physician, dentist, or podiatrist — a reminder that an opt-out only waives the federal Medicare condition; each state's own nurse practice act still sets the real ceiling on what a CRNA can do without physician involvement. Separately, Rhode Island's H7426, introduced January 30, would go a step further by removing the physician-supervision requirement outright for CRNAs with two or more years of experience; its current status in committee wasn't confirmed by available sources, so SRNAs in or near Rhode Island should track it directly rather than assume passage. One estimate puts the number of states (plus Washington, D.C.) where CRNAs can practice fully independently — distinct from the Medicare opt-out count — at 23, underscoring how much hospital bylaws and state board rules still vary even within opt-out states.

💡Knowledge Pearl

Sugammadex doesn't block the neuromuscular junction — it removes the blocker from circulation entirely. Rocuronium and vecuronium are steroidal nondepolarizing agents; sugammadex is a modified gamma-cyclodextrin, a ring-shaped sugar molecule with a lipophilic core sized almost perfectly to trap the steroidal nucleus of those two drugs. It encapsulates the free drug in plasma, forming a tight, essentially irreversible 1:1 complex that is then cleared renally — dropping free rocuronium/vecuronium concentration so fast that the neuromuscular junction clears on its own, without needing acetylcholinesterase inhibition.

That mechanism explains two testable quirks: sugammadex does not reverse benzylisoquinolinium agents like cisatracurium (wrong-shaped molecule, no encapsulation), and dosing is driven by train-of-four count rather than a flat dose — 2 mg/kg at reappearance of T2, 4 mg/kg for deep block (post-tetanic count 1–2), and 16 mg/kg for immediate reversal right after a rocuronium intubating dose.

🕰️This Week in Anesthesia History

Barely a month after William T.G. Morton's first public ether demonstration at Massachusetts General Hospital, junior surgeon Henry J. Bigelow stood before the Boston Society for Medical Improvement and formally reported on the run of successful ether anesthetics that had followed. Bigelow's account — soon expanded into his landmark paper in the Boston Medical and Surgical Journal — was one of the first serious clinical write-ups of the new technique, and it mattered enormously: it moved "ether anesthesia" from a dramatic one-off demonstration into something other surgeons could read about, evaluate, and start to replicate. The patent fight between Morton and his former partner Charles Jackson over who actually "discovered" ether anesthesia was already simmering by this point — but Bigelow's dry, careful case series is arguably what turned a sensational stunt into a medical practice.

🧠Board Question of the Week

A 34-year-old patient receiving an ultrasound-guided interscalene block for shoulder surgery becomes agitated and confused approximately 8 minutes after local anesthetic injection, then develops perioral numbness followed by a generalized tonic-clonic seizure. Heart rate is 38 bpm and trending down. Which of the following is the most appropriate next step in management, in addition to securing the airway and stopping the local anesthetic?

Correct answer: C — 20% lipid emulsion therapy

This presentation — CNS signs (agitation, perioral numbness, seizure) evolving into cardiovascular collapse (bradycardia progressing toward arrest) — is textbook local anesthetic systemic toxicity (LAST), almost certainly from intravascular uptake during the interscalene block. ASRA's LAST checklist calls for immediate lipid emulsion therapy as soon as LAST is suspected, not held in reserve for cardiac arrest: a 1.5 mL/kg bolus of 20% lipid emulsion over 2–3 minutes, followed by an infusion, works by acting as a "lipid sink" that pulls lipophilic local anesthetic out of cardiac and neural tissue.

Benzodiazepines (B) have a role for seizure control in LAST but don't address the underlying cardiotoxicity, and escalating the dose risks worsening cardiovascular depression — it's adjunctive, not the next most important step. If arrest occurs, ACLS doses of epinephrine (A) should be reduced (≤1 mcg/kg) because LAST hearts are especially prone to arrhythmia with standard-dose vasopressors; vasopressin is also avoided. Bradycardia here is a toxicity sign to treat at its source, not a primary pacing indication (D) — pacing an actively poisoned myocardium without addressing the LAST itself won't fix the underlying problem.
