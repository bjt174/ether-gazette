📰Headlines

A Pharmacy Mix-Up in Nashville Harms Four Patients, and Anesthesia's Safety Groups Respond

On August 14, four patients undergoing joint-replacement surgery at Ascension Saint Thomas Midtown in Nashville received potassium phosphate instead of their intended spinal anesthetic, mepivacaine, after a pharmacy compounding error; all four suffered adverse reactions. A week later, the Society for Obstetric Anesthesia and Perinatology, the Anesthesia Patient Safety Foundation, and ASA issued a joint statement reaffirming that properly administered spinal anesthesia remains safe and essential, while renewing calls for standardized, clearly labeled medications; physical separation of high-alert drugs from those intended for neuraxial use; and technology-assisted verification throughout pharmacy preparation, dispensing, and administration. The case has become a fast-moving case study in wrong-drug error prevention well beyond Nashville.

ASA · Anesthesiology Organizations Express Concern for Patients Harmed · APSF · Same Statement

CMS's Proposed 2027 Fee Schedule Would Cut Anesthesia Pay Again — the Comment Window Just Closed

CMS's proposed CY 2027 Medicare Physician Fee Schedule, released July 14 with a public comment period that closed September 14, would set conversion factors of $33.1693 for qualifying alternative-payment-model participants and $32.8409 for everyone else — reductions of roughly 1.2% and 1.7% from 2026 — as the temporary 2.5% statutory boost Congress added for this year expires. Anesthesia's specialty-specific practice-expense and malpractice adjustment offers only a 0.3% offset against that decline. A final rule is expected later this fall; for the second year running, hospital-based specialties including anesthesiology are bracing for real reimbursement pressure regardless of how CMS resolves the open comments.

Federal Register · CY 2027 Physician Fee Schedule Proposed Rule · Holland & Knight · CMS Issues CY 2027 PFS Proposed Rule

House Ways and Means Unanimously Advances a Bill to Send More Anesthesiologists to Rural Hospitals

The Medicare Access to Rural Anesthesiology Act (H.R. 9642) cleared the House Ways and Means Committee by unanimous vote in July and now awaits a floor vote. The bipartisan bill would add anesthesiologists to Medicare's existing rural pass-through payment program — open to other specialists but not anesthesiologists under current law — giving rural hospitals a Medicare-funded incentive to recruit and retain physician anesthesia coverage. ASA has pushed for the change since first introducing the bill in 2023, framing rural anesthesia access as fundamentally an economic and staffing problem rather than a scope-of-practice one.

📚From the Literature

A Perianesthetic "Bundle" Cut Postoperative Delirium by More Than Half After Thoracic Surgery

In a prospective, randomized, single-blind trial at a Chinese tertiary teaching hospital, 508 patients undergoing elective thoracic surgery with an expected anesthesia duration of at least two hours were assigned to routine perioperative care or to a multidisciplinary "bundle" adding structured perioperative pain control, rationalized medication selection, refined intraoperative management, and cognitive training. Postoperative delirium occurred in just 3.6% of the bundle group (9 of 247 patients) versus 9.2% of controls (23 of 250) — a difference that held up in both intention-to-treat and per-protocol analysis. For SRNAs, it's a useful reminder that delirium prevention is rarely about a single drug choice; it's the accumulation of small, coordinated perioperative decisions.

⚖️Legislation & Scope Tracker

CRNA Opt-Out Now Covers 32 States Plus D.C. — Up From 19 Just Twenty Months Ago

The count of states that have opted out of the federal physician-supervision requirement for CRNA Medicare reimbursement has climbed to 32 plus the District of Columbia, up from 19 in January 2025, according to industry practice-authority trackers. Opt-out status removes the federal supervision requirement but doesn't by itself grant full, independent state-law practice authority — several opt-out states still layer on collaborative-agreement or written-protocol requirements — so the figure is a ceiling on potential independence, not a count of CRNAs actually practicing unsupervised. The trend line has moved in one direction only for years.

Florida's Autonomous-CRNA-Practice Bill Dies in Committee — For This Session

HB 375 and its Senate companion, SB 462, which would have let qualified CRNAs register as autonomous advanced-practice nurses and drop Florida's written-protocol requirement with a physician, both died in committee on March 13 — HB 375 in Senate Rules, SB 462 in Health Policy. Neither bill reached a floor vote during the 2026 session. Florida joins a familiar pattern: opt-out and full-practice bills are increasingly common but far from guaranteed passage even within a single legislative cycle, and advocates on both sides expect a reintroduction next session.

💡Knowledge Pearl

Why sugammadex doesn't reverse cisatracurium — and why that matters when rocuronium is backordered. Sugammadex is a modified γ-cyclodextrin that reverses neuromuscular blockade by molecular encapsulation: its lipophilic core forms a tight 1:1 host–guest complex with the steroidal aminosteroid neuromuscular blockers rocuronium and, less efficiently, vecuronium, pulling free drug out of the neuromuscular junction and into plasma down a newly created concentration gradient. Cisatracurium and atracurium are benzylisoquinolinium blockers — a completely different molecular scaffold, lacking the steroid nucleus sugammadex's cavity is shaped to bind — so sugammadex has no meaningful reversal effect on them. With rocuronium supply under strain for years and Hospira's cisatracurium formulations now facing discontinuation, some centers are substituting between the two drug classes to preserve inventory; the substitution is not equivalent from a reversal standpoint.

Board-relevant pearl: neostigmine (with glycopyrrolate) remains the only practical reversal option for benzylisoquinolinium blockers like cisatracurium, and it has a ceiling effect that sugammadex doesn't — as an acetylcholinesterase inhibitor, neostigmine can only augment the acetylcholine already available at the junction, so reversing a deep block (TOF count 0–1) reliably requires some spontaneous recovery to already be underway. Sugammadex, by contrast, works independent of the patient's own recovery because it removes the blocking drug directly. This distinction — encapsulation versus enzyme inhibition — is one of the more commonly tested reversal-agent concepts on the boards, and it stops being academic the moment a formulary shortage forces a switch between drug classes mid-case.

🕰️This Week in Anesthesia History
September 22, 1818

On September 22, 1818, an anonymous article appeared in the Quarterly Journal of Science and the Arts describing the curious, lethargic stupor that could be produced by inhaling ether vapor. Its author was a 26-year-old laboratory assistant named Michael Faraday, working under Humphry Davy at the Royal Institution — the same Davy who, two decades earlier, had first proposed nitrous oxide's potential to "annihilate physical pain" during surgery, only to see the idea shelved for a generation. Faraday's brief note observed that ether vapor, like nitrous oxide, could produce effects resembling intoxication, and that its inhalation deserved further study — but like Davy's nitrous oxide observation before it, the finding sat unused for nearly three decades. It would take until October 16, 1846, and William Morton's public demonstration at Massachusetts General Hospital, for ether's anesthetic potential to move from a laboratory curiosity to the operating room. Faraday went on to a career defined by electromagnetism, not medicine, but his 1818 footnote remains one of the earliest documented observations of the drug that gave this newsletter its name.

🧠Board Question of the Week

A 34-year-old patient with a T6 spinal cord injury sustained 10 weeks ago presents for urgent debridement of a sacral pressure ulcer. The surgical team requests a rapid-sequence induction. Which of the following is the most appropriate neuromuscular blocking agent choice, and why?

Correct answer: B — a high-dose nondepolarizer is the standard alternative once succinylcholine is contraindicated

Succinylcholine depolarizes the neuromuscular junction by binding acetylcholine receptors. After denervation injuries such as spinal cord injury, extrajunctional (immature-type) nicotinic receptors proliferate across the muscle membrane beginning roughly 3–5 days post-injury and persisting for many months, dramatically increasing the receptor pool succinylcholine can depolarize simultaneously. This produces an exaggerated, potentially life-threatening potassium efflux — hyperkalemic cardiac arrest has been reported with routine succinylcholine dosing in this population. At 10 weeks post-injury, this patient is squarely within the high-risk window, so succinylcholine should be avoided regardless of surgical urgency. A high-dose, rapid-onset nondepolarizing agent such as rocuronium (1.2 mg/kg) is the standard alternative for RSI when succinylcholine is contraindicated, accepting a longer duration of action that can, if needed, be shortened with sugammadex reversal.

A is wrong because it inverts the actual risk pattern — succinylcholine's danger increases the further the injury is from truly hyperacute, not less. C is wrong because the vulnerable window begins days after injury, not within the first 24–48 hours, and can persist for many months; ten weeks is well within it. D is wrong because the answer's reasoning is an overly broad, untrue categorical claim — the relevant point isn't that nondepolarizers are "always" preferred for RSI, it's why succinylcholine specifically is dangerous in this patient.
