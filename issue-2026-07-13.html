📰Headlines

AANA Withdraws “Nurse Anesthesiologist” Trademark Bids After ASA Challenge

On June 29, the American Association of Nurse Anesthesiology voluntarily withdrew two pending trademark applications — for "American Association of Nurse Anesthesiologists" and a companion mark — after the American Society of Anesthesiologists challenged them, arguing "anesthesiologist" has denoted a physician since 1922. AANA president Jeff Molter was quick to frame the move as a paperwork decision, not a retreat: "AANA made a voluntary decision that has no impact on what CRNAs are called, how we practice, or the qualifications we bring to patient care." Expect the underlying title dispute to keep simmering in state legislatures even as the trademark fight itself closes.

Anthem Folds Moderate Sedation Payments Into Facility Fees Starting July 1

Anthem Blue Cross Blue Shield plans in Georgia, Indiana, Missouri, Nevada, New Hampshire, and Ohio stopped separately reimbursing facilities for moderate sedation as of July 1, bundling it into the primary procedure or facility payment instead. The insurer says the change mirrors CMS's outpatient prospective payment methodology and closes a "duplicate payment" gap; participating facilities are barred from balance-billing members for the difference. Anesthesia groups are watching closely for signs the policy migrates to other Anthem markets or gets mirrored by other commercial payers.

New Survey Finds Three in Four CRNAs Got a Raise — But Most Would Still Consider Leaving

A June 3 workforce report from TrueLearn's APEX Anesthesia Review, drawn from 634 CRNAs and SRNAs nationwide, found 77 percent saw a salary increase over the past year, with more than a third up over 10 percent; only 4 percent took a pay cut. Yet 60 percent said they're open to a new opportunity, and 58 percent would consider relocating for better pay or work-life balance — with 35 percent skipping any job posting that omits compensation details outright. The picture: a workforce being paid better, in a market too tight for employers to take retention for granted.

📚From the Literature

Remimazolam Beats Propofol on Hemodynamic Stability at Induction — In Hypertensive Patients, Specifically

A trial-sequential meta-analysis of randomized controlled trials found remimazolam induction associated with significantly less post-induction hypotension (RR 0.71) and bradycardia (RR 0.26) than propofol in hypertensive adults, along with a higher nadir mean arterial pressure and heart rate. The authors are careful to flag that trial sequential analysis remained inconclusive and that most included trials were small — this is a "promising signal," not yet practice-changing evidence — but it adds to a growing case for remimazolam as the gentler induction agent in patients who can least afford a blood-pressure dip.

Song, S. E., Kim, S. H., Jeong, S., Kang, H., & Kim, H. J. (2026). Hemodynamic stability with remimazolam versus propofol during anesthesia induction in hypertensive patients: A meta-analysis with trial sequential analysis of randomized controlled trials. Korean Journal of Anesthesiology, 79(3), 306–317. https://doi.org/10.4097/kja.25842

⚖️Legislation & Scope Tracker

South Carolina's H.4044/S.360 Would Strip CRNA Supervision Language, Add Prescriptive Authority

Companion bills introduced in the South Carolina House and Senate would remove the state's physician-supervision requirement for CRNAs and grant prescriptive authority for anesthesia-related medications. Both remain in committee as of this month, with no floor vote scheduled. If enacted, South Carolina would join the growing roster of states decoupling CRNA practice from mandatory physician oversight — though, as always, state scope law and Medicare's opt-out status are two separate questions that don't automatically move together.

Virginia's SB33 Targets Autonomy for CRNAs in Underserved Areas

Virginia's SB33, carried into the 2026 session, would relax supervision requirements specifically for CRNAs practicing in rural and underserved facilities — a narrower approach than the full-scope bills pursued elsewhere. Notably, Virginia law already leaves "supervision" undefined and does not require an on-site supervisor, so the bill's practical effect would be to formalize protections that some facilities already operate under informally. The measure remains in committee.

💡Knowledge Pearl

Why remimazolam clears the same in a healthy 30-year-old and a patient in renal failure. Unlike midazolam, which depends on hepatic CYP3A4, remimazolam is broken down by tissue esterases — the same organ-independent pathway that makes remifentanil so predictable. The result is rapid, consistent offset that doesn't accumulate in hepatic or renal impairment, and a context-sensitive half-time that barely rises with infusion duration.

It's still a benzodiazepine at its core: it binds the GABA-A receptor and is fully reversible with flumazenil, which is more than can be said for propofol. That combination — organ-independent clearance plus a rescue reversal agent — is why remimazolam keeps showing up in board questions about induction choices in the hemodynamically fragile patient.

🕰️This Week in Anesthesia History
July 15, 1868

William T. G. Morton, the Boston dentist who administered the first publicly demonstrated surgical ether anesthetic at Massachusetts General Hospital on October 16, 1846, died on this date — reportedly after collapsing during a carriage ride through New York's Central Park, having spent much of his later life in a bitter and financially ruinous fight over credit for the discovery. He never secured the recognition — or the patent royalties — he spent two decades pursuing; today the amphitheater where he administered that first ether anesthetic is preserved as the "Ether Dome," a National Historic Landmark.

🧠Board Question of the Week

A patient receives succinylcholine for rapid-sequence induction and remains apneic and unable to breathe spontaneously 90 minutes after a single intubating dose, with no other identifiable cause. A dibucaine number of 22 is subsequently reported. What does this finding indicate?

Correct answer: C — Homozygous atypical pseudocholinesterase

The dibucaine number reflects the percentage inhibition of pseudocholinesterase activity by dibucaine, not enzyme quantity or concentration. A normal homozygous genotype yields a dibucaine number around 70–80; heterozygotes typically fall in the 40–60 range with moderately prolonged block (20–30 minutes); homozygous atypical individuals score roughly 20 or below and can remain paralyzed for hours, since their enzyme binds dibucaine — and succinylcholine — poorly.

Management is supportive: sedation, continued mechanical ventilation, and reassurance that the block will resolve as succinylcholine is slowly hydrolyzed by non-specific esterases elsewhere in the body. Reversal agents have no role here, since there is no acetylcholinesterase-mediated neuromuscular blockade to reverse.
