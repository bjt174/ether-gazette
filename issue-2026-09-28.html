Early previews highlight studies suggesting ketamine given during cesarean delivery may ease early postpartum depression symptoms, that routine ibuprofen may lower postoperative delirium risk, that preoperative sleep aids are linked to less pain and lower opioid use afterward, and that cannabis use around the time of surgery may raise perioperative cardiac risk while lowering clot risk. Federal Workforce Analysts Put a Harder Number on the Anesthesia Shortage

HRSA's Bureau of Health Workforce, via its National Center for Health Workforce Analysis, now projects a shortage of roughly 3,720 anesthesiologist full-time-equivalents nationally this year, widening to about 8,140 by 2031 as demand for surgical and procedural volume keeps outpacing new supply. A parallel industry workforce analysis puts the metro-area CRNA shortfall at around 3,140 FTEs for 2026, with the gap expected to persist through the decade. Roughly 30% of practicing anesthesiologists are projected to leave clinical practice by 2033, and more than 80% of rural U.S. counties already lack a practicing anesthesiologist at all, leaning even more heavily on CRNAs to cover the gap. The doctoral-degree requirement that took effect for new CRNAs in 2025 has also stretched training timelines, adding pressure on the supply side just as demand keeps climbing.

AANA Presses CMS to Let CRNAs Share in Medicare's New Mandatory Joint-Replacement Payment Model

In a September 1 comment letter to CMS Administrator Mehmet Oz, AANA asked the agency to explicitly confirm that CRNAs and CRNA group practices can qualify as "collaborators" — and, by extension, as advanced-alternative-payment-model participants — under the proposed Comprehensive Care for Joint Replacement Expanded (CJR-X) model, a mandatory bundled-payment program CMS wants to extend to nearly every acute-care hospital performing hip and knee replacements starting October 2027. Collaborator status would let CRNA practices share in CJR-X gainsharing payments tied to cost and quality performance, and, if their hospital partner meets certified-EHR criteria, potentially count toward Qualifying APM Participant status — a designation with real financial stakes under Medicare's broader value-based payment push. AANA's letter argues nothing in CMS's proposed definition of "collaborator" should exclude nurse anesthesia practices.

📚From the Literature

A Meta-Analysis of 18 Trials Finds a Real but Modest Edge for Video Laryngoscopy in Critically Ill Adults

Pooling 18 randomized controlled trials totaling 5,097 critically ill adults undergoing tracheal intubation, this trial-sequential meta-analysis found video laryngoscopy improved first-attempt intubation success versus direct laryngoscopy (risk ratio 1.09; 95% CI, 1.01–1.17), with the benefit concentrated in ICU settings (RR 1.19) and largest when neuromuscular blocking agents were used (RR 1.11); prehospital settings showed no significant advantage. Time to intubation differed by only about 2.4 seconds — clinically negligible — and there were no meaningful differences in mortality or hospital length of stay. Notably, the authors' trial sequential analysis found the evidence base still falls short of the roughly 9,782 patients needed to call the first-attempt-success benefit conclusively proven, despite video laryngoscopy's now-widespread adoption. For SRNAs, it's a useful corrective: VL's advantage over DL in this population is real but smaller and less certain than routine practice might suggest, and where you intubate matters as much as what you intubate with.

BMC Anesthesiology · Efficacy of Video Laryngoscopy Versus Direct Laryngoscopy for Critically Ill Adults

Wang, Z., Luo, Z., Yan, M., & Xu, J. (2026). Efficacy of video laryngoscopy versus direct laryngoscopy for critically ill adults undergoing tracheal intubation: A trial sequential analysis and meta-analysis. BMC Anesthesiology, 26, Article 312. https://doi.org/10.1186/s12871-026-03788-x

⚖️Legislation & Scope Tracker

Rhode Island's Latest Attempt to Drop Physician Supervision for Experienced CRNAs Awaits a Floor Vote

Introduced January 30, H7426 would create a new "non-supervised CRNA" designation for nurse anesthetists with two or more years of post-certification practice, granting them independent authority to provide anesthesia care and issue prescriptions without a supervising physician, dentist, or podiatrist; CRNAs with less experience would remain classified as "supervised." The bill also directs Rhode Island's Executive Office of Health and Human Services to pursue any Medicaid waivers needed to guarantee reimbursement for the newly independent CRNAs, with an effective date of January 1, 2027 if enacted. It has not yet reached a floor vote in either chamber.

New York Remains the Only State Without a Statutory CRNA Scope of Practice — Again

S357, which would finally create a defined "nurse anesthetist" profession and scope of practice under New York's education law, remains pending in the Senate Higher Education Committee after being reintroduced for a third consecutive session, following earlier versions in 2021 (S5435B) and 2023 (S769A/B). In February testimony to the Senate, the New York State Association of Nurse Anesthetists argued it's time for New York to join the other 49 states in giving CRNAs a defined statutory title and scope, rather than leaving practice authority to rest on regulation and institutional bylaws. The bill carries broad co-sponsorship in both chambers but has not advanced to a floor vote in any prior session.

💡Knowledge Pearl

Why GLP-1 receptor agonists turned "how long has it been since you ate" into a harder question. Semaglutide, tirzepatide, and liraglutide don't just lower glucose and suppress appetite — they slow gastric emptying, largely through vagally mediated effects on antral motility and pyloric tone, as part of the same mechanism that produces satiety. That delay is dose- and duration-dependent and doesn't reliably resolve with routine preoperative fasting: unlike a normal stomach, a GLP-1-slowed stomach may still hold solid contents after a standard 8-hour fast, even in an asymptomatic patient with no nausea, reflux, or early satiety to flag the risk. Case reports of aspiration during endoscopy and elective surgery in patients who followed standard NPO guidance are what pushed ASA and partner societies to abandon their original 2023 advice (hold the drug for a fixed number of days) in favor of a 2024 risk-stratified approach.

Board-relevant pearl: the current multi-society guidance doesn't set a universal NPO extension — it asks the anesthesia team to screen for GI symptoms, consider point-of-care gastric ultrasound when available, and individualize the decision between proceeding, treating the patient as full-stomach (rapid-sequence induction or awake intubation), or delaying the case, rather than relying on days-since-last-dose alone. The underlying physiology — GLP-1 receptors slowing gastric motility through vagal and enteric pathways — is the testable concept; the clinical takeaway is that a reassuring fasting history doesn't rule out a full stomach in this population.

🕰️This Week in Anesthesia History
September 30, 1811

On September 30, 1811, the English novelist Fanny Burney underwent a mastectomy in Paris for suspected breast cancer — fully conscious, with no anesthetic of any kind, thirty-five years before Morton's ether demonstration would make such an operation unthinkable to endure awake. Burney refused wine or opium beforehand, fearing they would dull her ability to cooperate with the surgeons. Seven men, including her husband, held her down as Baron Dominique-Jean Larrey, Napoleon's chief military surgeon, performed the excision with a team of assistants; Burney later described feeling the blade "cutting through veins — arteries — flesh — nerves" in a letter to her sister written nearly a year later, one of the most detailed first-person surgical accounts to survive from the pre-anesthetic era. She lived another 29 years. Her account is still cited in the history of medicine as a visceral reminder of exactly what ether, and everything that followed it, was invented to end.

🧠Board Question of the Week

During rapid-sequence induction for an emergent case, two attempts at direct laryngoscopy fail to visualize the glottis. Mask ventilation is difficult but still possible, and oxygen saturation has fallen to 89%. A video laryngoscope is immediately available at the bedside. What is the most appropriate next step?

Correct answer: C — escalate to a different, higher-yield technique before moving to a surgical airway

This is a difficult airway with ongoing, if difficult, mask ventilation — not a "cannot intubate, cannot oxygenate" emergency. The ASA Difficult Airway Algorithm calls for limiting repeated attempts with a failing technique and escalating to a different device or approach while oxygenation can still be maintained. Video laryngoscopy improves glottic visualization by bypassing the need for a direct line of sight and, per recent meta-analysis data, modestly improves first-attempt success over direct laryngoscopy, particularly when a neuromuscular blocker is already on board — making it the logical next step here, before committing to an invasive surgical airway.

A is wrong because repeating the same failed technique without changing anything meaningfully lowers, not raises, the odds of success and burns time and oxygen reserve. B is wrong because a surgical airway is reserved for a true "cannot intubate, cannot oxygenate" scenario — mask ventilation, though difficult, is still working here, so a less invasive rescue option should be tried first. D is wrong because this patient cannot be safely left unsecured with a desaturating, difficult airway; the algorithm calls for active escalation, not watchful waiting.
