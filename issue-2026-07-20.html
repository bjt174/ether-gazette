📰Headlines

Ohio Tells CMS It No Longer Needs the Federal Physician-Supervision Rule for CRNAs

On July 10, Governor Mike DeWine notified the Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services that Ohio is opting out of the federal requirement that a physician supervise CRNA-administered anesthesia, writing that the mandate "is no longer needed" given the state's newly modernized nurse-anesthesia law. The certification follows House Bill 52, which DeWine signed in March and which took effect June 8, replacing Ohio's decades-old supervision language with a collaborative-practice model built around current CRNA training and standards. CMS has allowed governors to make this certification since 2001, and a growing roster of states has done so since — though, as always, opting out of the federal Medicare condition is a separate question from what each state's own nurse practice act permits.

A Widening Gap: Report Projects the U.S. Will Be Short 12,500 CRNAs by 2033

A workforce analysis making the rounds this month puts hard numbers on a familiar strain: the U.S. is on pace to be short roughly 12,500 CRNAs by 2033 — nearly 22 percent of today's workforce — as retirements accelerate faster than training programs can replace them. About 31 percent of practicing CRNAs are already 55 or older, while the nation's 155 accredited programs graduate only around 2,400 new CRNAs a year, barely enough to offset departures, let alone grow the pipeline. Rural facilities, which lean most heavily on CRNAs for anesthesia coverage, are expected to feel the shortfall first and hardest.

NorthStar's CEO and Chief Anesthetist Officer Talk Private Equity and the Next Decade of Anesthesia

In AANA's "State of Anesthesia: 2026" conversation, NorthStar Anesthesia CEO Adam Spiegel and Chief Anesthetist Officer Randy Moore, DNP, MBA, CRNA — who spent four years as AANA's own CEO before joining NorthStar — walked through what's reshaping the business of anesthesia: a tightening CRNA labor market, shifting hospital-employment dynamics, and the growing footprint of private equity in anesthesia staffing groups. The pair expanded on the same themes at this year's Annual Congress in a session titled "Anesthesia 2030: Business Models, Workforce Shifts, and the Future of Practice" — worth a look for anyone trying to read where employment models are headed before they graduate into one.

📚From the Literature

Bigger Trial, Same Old Answer: Propofol Doesn't Beat Volatile Anesthesia on Cancer Survival

The long-awaited GA-CARES trial — a pragmatic, five-center randomized trial testing whether propofol-based total intravenous anesthesia improves survival after resection of high-risk cancers (pancreas, esophagus, lung, stomach, bile duct, liver, bladder, or peritoneal surface) compared with volatile-agent maintenance — reported its results this year. The answer was no: two-year mortality ran slightly higher in the propofol arm (26.1%) than the volatile arm (22.9%), and the trial did not support the retrospective-data-driven hypothesis that propofol's anti-inflammatory and immunomodulatory properties translate into a survival benefit. For students who've absorbed a decade of buzz about propofol's theoretical oncologic edge, GA-CARES is a useful corrective: choose your maintenance agent on hemodynamics, PONV risk, and clinical context — not on an unproven cancer-outcomes argument.

⚖️Legislation & Scope Tracker

Mississippi's Medical Board Walks Back a Rule That Would Have Reshaped Rural Anesthesia Coverage

A proposed rule from the Mississippi State Board of Medical Licensure — filed last September — would have required a supervising surgeon to personally assume anesthesia decision-making, informed consent, and continuous availability whenever a CRNA administered anesthesia without an on-site anesthesiologist. After the comment period closed on October 25, opposition led by LifeLinc Anesthesia argued the rule conflicted with the state's own Nursing Practice Act, that 92 percent of surveyed rural surgeons said they'd refuse the new responsibilities outright, and that compliance could cost individual facilities $800,000 to $1.2 million a year — money many rural hospitals don't have. The Board withdrew the proposal, preserving Mississippi's existing collaborative-care model, at least for now.

The Federal ICAN Act Is Still Sitting in Committee — But Its Sponsor List Keeps Growing

The Improving Care and Access to Nurses (ICAN) Act — H.R. 1317 in the House, S. 575 in the Senate — was reintroduced in February 2025 by Reps. Dave Joyce (R-OH), Suzanne Bonamici (D-OR), Lauren Underwood (D-IL), and Jen Kiggans (R-VA), with Sens. Jeff Merkley (D-OR) and Cynthia Lummis (R-WY) carrying the Senate companion. The bill would permanently remove Medicare's physician-supervision requirement for CRNAs nationwide, secure direct reimbursement, and extend ordering and referral authority — a federal analog to what states like Ohio just did unilaterally through the opt-out process. Neither bill has moved out of committee, but AANA continues to report incremental gains in bipartisan cosponsors as the 119th Congress heads toward its second-year home stretch.

💡Knowledge Pearl

Why 20% lipid emulsion reverses local anesthetic cardiotoxicity. Local anesthetic systemic toxicity (LAST) starts with CNS signs — perioral numbness, tinnitus, a metallic taste, agitation — and can progress within minutes to seizures and, in the worst cases, cardiovascular collapse that's notoriously resistant to standard ACLS. Bupivacaine is the classic villain: highly lipophilic and tightly protein-bound, it partitions into cardiac tissue and blocks myocardial sodium channels longer and more potently than less lipophilic agents like lidocaine or ropivacaine.

The fix is almost as counterintuitive as the injury: 20% IV lipid emulsion, given as a 1.5 mL/kg bolus followed by a 0.25 mL/kg/min infusion. The leading explanation is the "lipid sink" — an expanded intravascular lipid phase that pulls lipophilic local anesthetic molecules out of cardiac tissue and plasma faster than they can act — alongside a proposed direct metabolic and inotropic effect on stressed myocardium. Per ASRA's LAST checklist, propofol is not an acceptable substitute for lipid emulsion (its lipid content is too low and its cardiodepressant effects work against you), and standard resuscitation doses of epinephrine should be reduced, since large boluses appear to worsen outcomes in LAST-associated arrest.

🕰️This Week in Anesthesia History
July 25, 1834

On this date, the poet Samuel Taylor Coleridge died in London at 61 — best remembered for "Kubla Khan" and "The Rime of the Ancient Mariner," but also, decades earlier, an eager volunteer in one of anesthesia's true prehistory experiments. In 1799, Coleridge was among the writers and thinkers who gathered at Thomas Beddoes's Pneumatic Institution in Bristol to inhale a curious new gas prepared by a young lab assistant named Humphry Davy: nitrous oxide. Coleridge described a sensation of "highly pleasurable thrilling" throughout his limbs, and Davy — who nearly injured himself testing gases on his own body throughout the project — went on to note, almost as an aside in his 1800 research volume, that nitrous oxide "appears capable of destroying physical pain" and might be used "with advantage during surgical operations." Forty-seven years would pass before anyone acted on that observation.

🧠Board Question of the Week

During an ultrasound-guided interscalene block, a patient becomes agitated and reports a metallic taste and ringing in her ears about 60 seconds after the local anesthetic is injected. Before the block needle is even withdrawn, she has a witnessed generalized tonic-clonic seizure. After securing the airway and terminating the seizure, which intervention should be initiated next?

Correct answer: B — 20% intravenous lipid emulsion

This presentation — early neurologic symptoms rapidly progressing to seizure right after a large-volume peripheral nerve block — is textbook local anesthetic systemic toxicity (LAST), most likely from inadvertent intravascular injection or rapid systemic absorption. ASRA's LAST checklist calls for airway management, seizure suppression with a benzodiazepine, and 20% lipid emulsion as first-line treatment: a 1.5 mL/kg bolus (roughly 100 mL in an average adult), repeatable up to twice for persistent cardiovascular collapse, followed by an infusion at 0.25 mL/kg/min until hemodynamic stability is restored.

Propofol is specifically discouraged as a lipid-emulsion substitute — its lipid concentration is too low to produce a meaningful sink effect, and its cardiodepressant properties can worsen an already unstable patient. Standard ACLS epinephrine doses should be reduced (to boluses of ≤1 mcg/kg) rather than given at full resuscitation doses, since case and animal data suggest large doses impair resuscitation from LAST-associated arrest. Calcium has no established role in LAST management; it treats hyperkalemia and calcium-channel-blocker toxicity, not local anesthetic cardiotoxicity.
