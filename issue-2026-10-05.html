📰Headlines

ASA Tells CMS Its 2027 Payment Proposals Would Deepen a "Fundamentally Broken" Reimbursement System

In formal comments filed September 14, the American Society of Anesthesiologists pushed back hard on CMS's proposed CY 2027 Medicare Physician Fee Schedule, which would cut the anesthesia conversion factor by roughly 0.9–1.4% depending on APM participation status. ASA asked CMS to withdraw a proposed 50% payment reduction for same-day services billed with Modifier 25, sought clearer anesthesia-specific practice-expense and malpractice adjustments, and pressed for at least a one-year delay of the untested Ambulatory Specialty Model before its scheduled January launch. The society also opposed a planned rollback of anesthesia-specific quality measures, including one tracking postoperative nausea and vomiting prevention. Final CY 2027 regulations are expected around November 1, with any changes effective January 1, 2027.

ASA · ASA Challenges Flawed Payment Proposals From CMS for 2027

GE HealthCare Issues an Urgent Correction for Aisys/Datex-Ohmeda Machines After Anesthetic Delivery Interruptions

The FDA has classified a GE HealthCare correction for Aisys and Datex-Ohmeda anesthesia delivery systems as a Class 2 recall (initiated July 30, posted to the FDA database September 16). Affected units contain electronic gas-mixer assemblies that can automatically transition the machine into Alternate O2 Control: the system keeps delivering 100% oxygen and audible/visual alarms sound, but volatile anesthetic agent delivery stops until a clinician intervenes. GE attributes the defect to a nonconforming component and has notified customers directly; the agency warns that an unrecognized transition could leave a patient under-anesthetized mid-case. Facilities running affected Aisys/Datex-Ohmeda units should confirm they've received and actioned GE's correction letter.

FDA · Class 2 Device Recall, Aisys/Datex-Ohmeda (Z-3189-2026)

A Fight Over Insurer Time Limits on Anesthesia Coverage Spreads Well Beyond Missouri

Missouri Governor Mike Kehoe's July 13 signature on House Bill 2372 — which bars health plans from capping anesthesia reimbursement by a predetermined surgical time limit — closed out a fight that began when Anthem Blue Cross Blue Shield floated exactly such a policy for Missouri, New York, and Connecticut in late 2024 before withdrawing it under pressure. Missouri joins Maryland and Illinois, which had already enacted similar bans, and the model is still spreading: Washington reintroduced its own version in late January, and New Jersey lawmakers have filed a companion bill. ASA and state medical societies are tracking the pattern as a leading indicator of where payers may try similar caps next.

📚From the Literature

A Large Substudy of POISE-3 Finds No Clear Cognitive Edge to Either Blood-Pressure Strategy After Noncardiac Surgery

The cogPOISE-3 substudy randomized 2,603 patients on chronic antihypertensives (age >45, no dementia) undergoing noncardiac surgery across 54 centers in 19 countries to a hypotension-avoidance strategy (intraoperative MAP target ≥80 mmHg, renin-angiotensin-system inhibitors withheld) or a hypertension-avoidance strategy (MAP target ≥60 mmHg, home antihypertensives continued). Postoperative delirium occurred in 7.3% of the hypotension-avoidance group versus 7.0% of the hypertension-avoidance group (RR 1.04; 95% CI, 0.79–1.38) — no meaningful difference. At one year, clinically significant cognitive decline (a drop of more than two points on the Montreal Cognitive Assessment) was seen in 37.2% versus 33.1% (RR 1.13; 95% CI, 0.92–1.38), also not statistically significant. For SRNAs, the practical takeaway is that neither strategy has been shown to meaningfully protect cognition in this population — so the choice of intraoperative blood-pressure target for a patient on chronic antihypertensives should still rest on hemodynamic and surgical considerations rather than an assumed neuroprotective edge.

Annals of Internal Medicine · Marcucci, M., Devereaux, P. J., & the cogPOISE-3 Trial Investigators and Study Groups. (2025). Effects of a hypotension-avoidance versus a hypertension-avoidance strategy on neurocognitive outcomes after noncardiac surgery. Annals of Internal Medicine, 178(7), 909–920. https://doi.org/10.7326/ANNALS-24-02841

Published July 2025 — just over a year old, well within the five-year window, but not brand-new; it's a secondary analysis of the earlier POISE-3 trial cohort.

⚖️Legislation & Scope Tracker

Louisiana Requires Payment Parity for Independently Practicing CRNAs

Act 257 (SB 275), signed by the governor May 15 after passing the House 96–0, requires health insurers to reimburse CRNAs practicing without physician medical direction — typically billed with the QZ modifier — at no less than the rate paid to a physician for the identical service, and bars insurers from excluding CRNAs from network participation solely on the basis of license type. The law was a direct response to a UnitedHealthcare policy that cut QZ-modifier reimbursement by 15%. It takes effect January 1, 2027.

Louisiana Legislature · SB 275 / Act 257 (2026)

Maryland's Anesthesiologist Assistant Licensing Law Takes Effect This Week

HB 1558 and its cross-filed companion SB 951, creating a new anesthesiologist assistant license under the Maryland Board of Physicians, take effect October 1, 2026. An AA license will permit the holder to perform specified anesthesia-related acts under an anesthesiologist's direct supervision, but the Board doesn't have to actually begin accepting license applications yet — it has until October 1, 2027 to finalize the implementing regulations and set an application start date. Maryland becomes the latest in a growing list of states licensing AAs as a distinct provider type alongside CRNAs.

💡Knowledge Pearl

Why remifentanil "wears off the same" no matter how long the infusion has run, and fentanyl doesn't. Context-sensitive half-time is the time needed for plasma drug concentration to fall by 50% after stopping an infusion, as a function of how long ("the context") the infusion has run — and it behaves very differently from a drug's elimination half-life. Fentanyl's context-sensitive half-time climbs steeply with infusion duration because the drug keeps redistributing into a large, slowly-equilibrating peripheral compartment (fat and muscle) that becomes an ever-larger reservoir the longer the infusion continues; after a few hours, fentanyl's effective offset can rival that of a drug with a much longer terminal half-life.

Remifentanil is the exception that makes the concept click: its context-sensitive half-time stays flat at roughly 3–5 minutes regardless of infusion duration, because it's metabolized by nonspecific plasma and tissue esterases rather than redistribution or hepatic clearance — the same enzymatic pathway keeps clearing it at a constant rate no matter how much drug has accumulated in peripheral tissue. That's the pharmacologic basis for using remifentanil infusions in cases where rapid, predictable emergence matters, and for the clinical reality that a remifentanil infusion must be backed up with longer-acting analgesia before it's stopped, or the patient will be in significant pain within minutes.

🕰️This Week in Anesthesia History
October 6, 1905

On October 6, 1905, Dr. A. F. Erdmann invited a small group of physicians to a meeting at Long Island College Hospital in Brooklyn, New York, to form the Long Island Society of Anesthetists — the first organized anesthesiology society in the United States, and only the second in the world after London's, founded in 1893. Eight physicians joined at a dollar a year, meeting monthly to discuss what was still, six decades after Ether Day, a largely improvised and under-studied branch of medicine, often assigned to whoever on the surgical team was most expendable. The society renamed itself the New York Society of Anesthetists in 1911, then the American Society of Anesthetists in 1936, and finally the American Society of Anesthesiologists in 1945 — the direct institutional ancestor of the group that will fill the San Diego Convention Center later this month for ANESTHESIOLOGY 2026. A hundred and twenty-one years on, the throughline from eight physicians in a Brooklyn hospital room to a 1,000-abstract annual meeting is worth a moment's notice.

🧠Board Question of the Week

Twelve minutes after an ultrasound-guided interscalene brachial plexus block with 30 mL of 0.5% bupivacaine, a patient becomes acutely confused, then develops perioral numbness followed by a generalized tonic-clonic seizure. Oxygen saturation is 94% on room air and the patient is rapidly becoming bradycardic. Which of the following is the most appropriate immediate next step?

Correct answer: B — this is local anesthetic systemic toxicity (LAST), and lipid emulsion is first-line treatment

The timing, CNS prodrome (confusion, perioral numbness), seizure, and cardiovascular instability following a large-volume bupivacaine block are classic for LAST — bupivacaine's high lipid solubility and potent sodium- and potassium-channel blockade make it especially prone to producing both seizures and malignant dysrhythmias when it reaches systemic circulation in sufficient concentration. Per ASRA's LAST checklist, management is airway management and oxygenation first (hypoxia and acidosis worsen local anesthetic cardiotoxicity), then prompt 20% lipid emulsion bolus (1.5 mL/kg lean body mass) followed by infusion, with ACLS modified to favor smaller epinephrine doses and to avoid vasopressin, calcium-channel blockers, and beta-blockers if cardiac arrest develops.

A is incomplete: treating the seizure alone ignores the cardiovascular toxicity still building. C misreads the presentation — there's no indication of opioid exposure, and naloxone does nothing for LAST. D is actively harmful, since it only adds more local anesthetic to a patient already showing systemic toxicity.
