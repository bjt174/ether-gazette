📰Headlines

AANA's New Workforce Report Warns of Nearly 8,000 CRNA Retirements by 2027

The American Association of Nurse Anesthesiology's "State of Anesthesia: 2026 Edition" lays out the workforce math SRNAs are training into: roughly 12% of the nation's approximately 70,000 practicing CRNAs and nurse anesthesiologists are projected to retire by 2027 — about 8,000 providers, or roughly 2,700 a year through the 2025–2027 window — with 49.2% of the current workforce already 50 or older. The training pipeline is not close to keeping pace: more than 150 accredited nurse anesthesia programs graduate around 2,400 new CRNAs annually, a number that mostly offsets attrition rather than closing the gap against rising surgical demand. The physician side of the house faces a parallel squeeze — federal workforce analysts project an anesthesiologist shortage of 3,720 FTEs this year, widening to 8,140 by 2031, with 57% of practicing anesthesiologists already over 55. For students finishing clinical training in the next few years, the report's subtext is double-edged: strong job market and leverage on one hand, thinner and more senior-heavy care teams to staff on the other.

AANA Ushers In New President Tracy Young and Launches a Patient-Facing "Anesthesia Answers" Site

AANA closed out its Annual Congress season with two institutional moves worth noting. On August 22, the association launched Anesthesia Answers, a new patient-education website at AnesthesiaAnswers.org built by CRNAs and nurse anesthesiologists to explain, in plain language, what anesthesia care involves and who provides it — a direct-to-consumer push that lands amid the ongoing, sometimes contentious "nurse anesthesiologist" title debate with ASA. Three days later, on August 25, AANA named Tracy Young, MSNA, MBA, CRNA, as its 2026–27 president. Young, a Louisiana-based CRNA since 2001 and co-founder and chief operating officer of Essential Anesthesia Management, succeeds the outgoing president for a term running through August 2027. Neither move changes practice authority anywhere, but together they signal where the association is investing this cycle: public-facing messaging and continuity of the group's advocacy leadership heading into ANESTHESIOLOGY 2026 and next year's legislative sessions.

ASC Anesthesia Stipends Keep Climbing as Physician Pay Tops Seven Figures for the First Time

Ambulatory surgery centers are paying more than ever to keep anesthesia coverage in place. The share of ASC leaders expecting to pay anesthesia stipends jumped from 28% in 2024 to 44% in 2025, and 60% now rank anesthesia coverage among their top three financial challenges heading into next year, according to Becker's ASC's ongoing survey coverage. More than 80% of ASCs now expect to lean on independent-contractor anesthesia arrangements in some form — 44% pairing contractors with a stipend, another 36% without one — as the traditional employed-group model keeps losing ground to a fee-plus-subsidy structure. The clearest signal of how tight the market has gotten: advertised W-2 base pay for physician anesthesiologists crossed seven figures for the first time in the first quarter of 2026. For SRNAs, the same dynamics that are straining ASC budgets are what's keeping new-graduate offers competitive — worth watching whether stipend growth holds once surgical centers start pushing back on the cost.

Comments Close Today on CMS's Second Straight Anesthesia Pay Cut — What Happens Next

The public comment period this newsletter has been tracking since August 17 closes today. CMS's Calendar Year 2027 Medicare Physician Fee Schedule proposed rule would set anesthesia conversion factors at $20.4165 per unit for Advanced Alternative Payment Model participants and $20.2143 for everyone else — declines of roughly 0.9% and 1.4% from 2026. The cut is compounded by a broader structural change: the temporary 2.5% statutory conversion-factor bump that applied through the end of calendar year 2026 expires on schedule, pulling the overall physician conversion factors down 1.19% (APM) and 1.68% (non-APM) even before anesthesia's own specialty-specific adjustment is applied. With the comment window now shut, CMS moves toward a final rule expected around November 1 — the same timeline on which last year's CY 2026 rule was finalized. Anything filed today is the last formal input stakeholders get before that number becomes real for 2027 billing.

📚From the Literature

A Large UK Biobank Study Ties a Dementia Risk Gene to Postoperative Delirium

Using genome-wide association analysis across 230,179 non-cardiac and 21,254 cardiac surgery patients in the UK Biobank, researchers identified APOE — the best-established genetic risk locus for Alzheimer's disease — as a genetic risk factor for ICD-coded postoperative delirium after non-cardiac surgery, and confirmed that patients who experienced postoperative delirium had significantly higher subsequent rates of dementia diagnosis. It's the largest genetic-epidemiology study to date connecting a specific, well-characterized dementia-risk gene to delirium risk in the perioperative setting, reinforcing a hypothesis anesthesia researchers have circled for years: that postoperative delirium isn't just a transient bedside nuisance, but may in some patients be an early marker of — or even a contributor to — longer-term neurodegenerative decline. The key caveat is ascertainment: the delirium cases were identified via hospital ICD coding rather than systematic bedside screening (e.g., CAM-ICU), a method well known to undercount true delirium incidence, which could bias the genetic associations in either direction. Still, for SRNAs, it's a useful reminder that "the patient's a little confused overnight, it'll clear" deserves the same seriousness as any other neurologic finding — the genetics increasingly suggest it isn't always background noise.

Ding, K., Wei, X., Tao, Y., Chen, S., Zhang, C., Chen, R., Chen, H., Han, X., Song, J., Liu, J., Zhang, S., & Chen, C. (2026). Genetic contributors to postoperative delirium and their implications for dementia outcomes. Anesthesiology, 145(4), 857–871. https://doi.org/10.1097/ALN.0000000000006191

⚖️Legislation & Scope Tracker

Wisconsin's CRNA Independent-Practice Provisions Take Effect This Month

Governor Tony Evers signed the APRN Modernization Act — 2025 Wisconsin Act 17 — into law on August 8, creating a new independent-practice license category covering nurse practitioners, CRNAs, certified nurse-midwives, and clinical nurse specialists. The law's independent-practice provisions take effect this September, meaning qualifying Wisconsin CRNAs can now begin practicing outside a mandatory collaborative relationship with a physician or dentist, provided they've completed the law's required combination of education and supervised practice hours. The act keeps a few guardrails in place: APRNs performing invasive pain-management procedures must either partner with a physician who specializes in pain medicine or practice within a hospital or hospital-affiliated clinic. Wisconsin joins a national trend that has moved in one direction only — no state has ever reversed a full-practice-authority decision after implementation — though exact national counts vary by tracker depending on how partial opt-outs (like Utah and Wyoming's rural-only carve-outs) are counted; one industry tally puts full practice authority at 31 states plus D.C. as of this year. For SRNAs, Wisconsin's phase-in — hours-based rather than automatic on the signing date — is a useful template for what "independent practice" actually requires in practice, not just on paper.

💡Knowledge Pearl

Why some local anesthetics numb a nerve in two minutes and others take fifteen: it comes down to pKa. Local anesthetics are weak bases that exist in solution as an equilibrium between a charged (protonated, ionized) form and an uncharged (nonionized) form. Only the uncharged form is lipid-soluble enough to cross the nerve's lipid membrane in meaningful quantity — so the closer a drug's pKa sits to physiologic pH (7.4), the larger its nonionized fraction at injection, and the faster its clinical onset. Lidocaine (pKa ≈7.9) and mepivacaine (pKa ≈7.6) act quickly for exactly this reason, while bupivacaine (pKa ≈8.1) is comparatively slower. Once inside the axoplasm, the drug re-ionizes and it's the charged form that actually plugs the intracellular gate of the voltage-gated sodium channel, blocking Na⁺ influx and preventing action-potential propagation — so onset speed and the actual blocking mechanism depend on two different forms of the same molecule.

Board-relevant pearl: potency and lipid solubility travel together (more lipid-soluble agents partition more readily into the nerve membrane and into cardiac tissue), and it's exactly this property that makes bupivacaine more cardiotoxic than lidocaine at equipotent doses. Bupivacaine binds cardiac sodium channels with markedly slower dissociation kinetics — the classic "fast-in, slow-out" description — so it accumulates in the channel between heartbeats at clinically relevant heart rates, producing refractory ventricular arrhythmias that are disproportionately hard to resuscitate compared with lidocaine toxicity. That pharmacologic detail is the entire reason ASRA's local anesthetic systemic toxicity (LAST) checklist treats bupivacaine-associated cardiac arrest differently from a routine ACLS code.

🕰️This Week in Anesthesia History
September 15, 1884

On September 15, 1884, the world learned that cocaine could numb a nerve. Carl Koller, a young Viennese ophthalmology resident, had spent weeks searching for a way to anesthetize the eye for surgery, after a colleague — Sigmund Freud, then pursuing cocaine's psychiatric potential — mentioned the drug's numbing effect on the tongue. Koller dissolved cocaine in water, dripped it into a frog's eye, then a rabbit's, then his own, and confirmed he could touch the cornea with a pin and feel nothing. Too poor to afford travel to the Heidelberg Ophthalmological Society's congress, Koller had a colleague, Josef Brettauer, read his findings to the assembled ophthalmologists on his behalf. The report ignited immediate excitement across surgical specialties — within months, surgeons worldwide were experimenting with cocaine for nerve blocks, and within a decade the search for a less toxic, non-addictive alternative had begun, eventually yielding procaine and the modern amide and ester local anesthetics SRNAs use today. Koller's own reward was more complicated: he emigrated to New York in 1888 after a duel over the priority dispute, and cocaine's addictive and cardiotoxic properties — including Freud's own struggles with it — became one of the field's earliest lessons in taking a "safe" new drug's dangers seriously.

🧠Board Question of the Week

Fifteen minutes into an ultrasound-guided interscalene block using 30 mL of 0.5% bupivacaine, a patient reports circumoral numbness and tinnitus, then becomes acutely agitated and has a witnessed generalized seizure. Shortly after, the patient becomes bradycardic and hypotensive. In addition to airway management and seizure control, which of the following is the most appropriate immediate treatment?

Correct answer: B — 20% lipid emulsion is first-line therapy for local anesthetic systemic toxicity

This presentation — perioral numbness and tinnitus progressing to seizure, followed by cardiovascular collapse — is the classic evolution of local anesthetic systemic toxicity (LAST) from intravascular or excessive systemic absorption of a large-volume block, and bupivacaine's slow dissociation from cardiac sodium channels makes it especially dangerous once cardiotoxicity begins. ASRA's LAST checklist calls for immediate 20% lipid emulsion — an initial bolus followed by an infusion — on top of airway management and seizure control, based on the "lipid sink" mechanism in which the emulsion sequesters the lipophilic local anesthetic away from cardiac tissue and may also improve myocardial energy substrate delivery.

A is wrong because ASRA specifically recommends reducing individual epinephrine doses (to ≤1 mcg/kg) during LAST resuscitation rather than giving standard ACLS boluses, since large doses of epinephrine have been associated with worse outcomes and impaired lipid resuscitation in animal models of bupivacaine toxicity. C is wrong because calcium channel blockers and beta blockers are specifically avoided in LAST — they compound the myocardial depression already caused by the local anesthetic. D is wrong and dangerous: giving additional local anesthetic during an evolving toxic reaction adds more drug to an already toxic systemic load.
