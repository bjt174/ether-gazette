📰Headlines

The Huons Ampule Recall Widens Again — This Time It's the Saline

The contamination chain this newsletter has tracked since July (our July 27, August 17, and August 24 issues) has moved past bupivacaine and lidocaine ampules to a new component: sodium chloride flush ampules made by Huons Co. and recalled by Elevaris Medical Devices. In an August 10 letter, Becton Dickinson told customers that certain BD convenience kits and procedure trays may contain the recalled saline, warning that compromised sterility could expose patients to viable microorganisms, endotoxins, or manufacturing debris capable of triggering systemic inflammatory response, infection, microclots, or organ failure. The FDA logged fresh early alerts for Medline and AVID Medical kits on August 24 covering the same underlying saline lot, alongside a Medical Action Industries epidural-kit alert — meaning five separate kit manufacturers are now untangling their supply chains from one contaminated ampule source. As of BD's July 29 count, no serious injuries had been reported from the saline specifically. The practical takeaway hasn't changed from prior issues: check incoming kits and trays against the FDA's lot list by component, not just by kit SKU, since the same manufacturer keeps resurfacing in new products months after the first correction.

A New Union Coalition Sues Over the Same Nursing Loan-Cap Rule a Judge Already Blocked

The fight over graduate nursing students' federal loan limits — including CRNA students — isn't over just because a preliminary injunction paused it (our August 3 issue). On August 11, the AFL-CIO, AFSCME, the American Federation of Teachers, and National Nurses United filed a separate suit in U.S. District Court for DC challenging the Department of Education's RISE rule, which would cap graduate nursing borrowing at $20,500 a year and $100,000 aggregate — half the $200,000 aggregate ceiling available to law, medicine, and other programs the rule classifies as "professional." The RISE rule itself narrowed an open-ended "professional degree" test into a closed list of eleven programs, excluding MSN and DNP nursing, along with social work, public health, and several allied-health fields. That case is now consolidated with the earlier litigation on an expedited summary-judgment track, with briefing set to close December 4 — meaning the loan-cap question stays unsettled well into this school year even with the injunction still in place.

WSNA · ANA and 9 Nursing Organizations Sue Federal Government Over Loan Limits

A New Report Puts This Year's Anesthesiologist Shortfall Near 3,700 Full-Time Positions

Medicus Healthcare Solutions' 2026 anesthesia clinician shortage report — a follow-up data point to the workforce trends this newsletter covered August 17 — estimates a current national shortfall of roughly 3,720 full-time-equivalent anesthesiologists, alongside a parallel 3,140 FTE CRNA shortage concentrated in metro markets. The demographic pressure behind the number is stark: 59% of practicing anesthesiologists are 55 or older, 17% are near retirement age, and more than 42% say they plan to leave their current role within two years. On the ground, that's translating into real financial strain for ambulatory surgery centers — the share expecting to pay anesthesia stipends jumped from 28% in 2024 to 44% in 2025, and two-thirds now list anesthesia coverage among their top three financial concerns. For SRNAs finishing clinicals over the next year or two, the market read is unchanged from prior issues: demand is outpacing supply almost everywhere outside the most saturated metros.

A New Multidisciplinary Consensus Backs Away From the Blanket SGLT2-Inhibitor Preop Hold

Two weeks after this newsletter flagged the tension between SGLT2 inhibitors' renal benefits and their euglycemic-ketoacidosis risk (our August 17 Literature pick), a 2026 multidisciplinary consensus from the Society for Perioperative Assessment and Quality Improvement (SPAQI) has weighed in on the side of nuance rather than a blanket rule. Rather than uniformly stopping SGLT2 inhibitors three to four days before every case, the consensus recommends individualizing the hold based on diabetes status, surgical complexity, expected fasting duration, and comorbid heart failure or chronic kidney disease — weighing the drugs' cardiorenal protection against ketoacidosis risk case by case. It's a meaningful shift from the fixed-interval guidance most programs still have printed on their preop instructions, and it lines up with data cited in APSF's own recent commentary on the topic questioning whether the traditional hold window is longer than the risk actually requires.

📚From the Literature

A 320-Patient Trial Finds Fewer Complications With Opioid-Free Cardiac Anesthesia

The OFACAR randomized clinical trial, run at two French tertiary university hospitals, assigned 320 adults undergoing elective on-pump cardiac surgery to either an opioid-free multimodal regimen (ketamine, dexamethasone, lidocaine, and magnesium sulfate) or a conventional sufentanil-based opioid technique. The primary composite outcome — 30-day neurologic, respiratory, cardiovascular, or renal complications, or death — occurred in 75.4% of the opioid-free group versus 84.5% of the opioid group (risk ratio 0.90; 95% CI, 0.80–0.99; P = 0.049), driven mainly by fewer cardiovascular and digestive complications; no deaths occurred in the opioid-free arm versus six in the control arm. The authors are notably cautious about their own result: the primary-outcome fragility index means a small number of outcome switches would have erased statistical significance, so they frame OFACAR as hypothesis-generating rather than practice-changing until a larger trial confirms it. Worth reading before you assume "opioid-free" automatically means "safer" in cardiac cases — the signal here is real but thin.

Guinot, P.-G., et al. (2026). Opioid-free anesthesia in cardiac surgery: The OFACAR randomized clinical trial. Anesthesiology. Advance online publication. https://doi.org/10.1097/ALN.0000000000006164

⚖️Legislation & Scope Tracker

Three of the Largest Supervision-Required States Show No Movement This Session

Mississippi, Tennessee, and Virginia remain physician-supervision states that have not opted out of the federal CMS supervision requirement, leaving the national opt-out count at 37 states plus DC — unchanged since our July 20 Ohio coverage — with 29 states plus DC granting CRNAs full independent practice authority outright. Virginia came closest to changing that picture: a 2025 bill to eliminate the state's direct-supervision requirement for CRNAs was introduced but didn't reach a floor vote, and sponsors have signaled they intend to reintroduce similar language in the next session rather than let it lapse quietly, the way Rhode Island's H7426 did in June (our August 17 issue). Mississippi and Tennessee show no comparable bill currently active. For SRNAs weighing where to practice after graduation, the reminder is the same one this column keeps making: "opted out federally" and "practices independently" are not the same status, and the gap between them is still wide in more than a dozen states.

💡Knowledge Pearl

Dexmedetomidine's sedative effect works through the same pathway as natural sleep — which is exactly why it doesn't suppress breathing the way other sedatives do. Dexmedetomidine is a highly selective α2-adrenergic agonist — roughly eight times more α2-selective than clonidine — that acts primarily at the locus coeruleus in the brainstem. Stimulating α2 receptors there hyperpolarizes noradrenergic neurons, shutting down the tonic norepinephrine output that normally keeps the cortex aroused, which disinhibits the ventrolateral preoptic nucleus and produces a sedative state that resembles natural non-REM sleep far more closely than a GABAergic drug like propofol does. Because the sedation isn't routed through GABA-A receptors, ventilatory drive is largely preserved even at clinically effective doses — the main reason dexmedetomidine has become a favored adjunct for awake fiberoptic intubation and sedation cases where spontaneous ventilation and airway reflexes need to stay intact.

Board-relevant pearl: know dexmedetomidine's biphasic hemodynamic signature. A loading dose can transiently activate peripheral postsynaptic α2B receptors on vascular smooth muscle, producing brief hypertension with reflex bradycardia, before the drug's dominant central α2A-mediated sympatholysis takes over and produces the more familiar steady-state hypotension and bradycardia. On an exam — or at the bedside — a patient who gets hypertensive right after a dexmedetomidine bolus isn't having a paradoxical reaction; that's the expected peripheral phase, and it's a reason to load slowly (or skip the bolus) in patients who can't tolerate the swing.

🕰️This Week in Anesthesia History
September 7, 1922

On September 7, 1922, William Stewart Halsted died in Baltimore — remembered today as the father of the American surgical residency system, but also, less famously, one of the founders of regional anesthesia. In 1884, after reading Carl Koller's report that cocaine could numb the eye for ophthalmic surgery, Halsted and several colleagues began injecting cocaine directly into peripheral nerves on themselves and each other, systematically mapping out what became the first true nerve-block techniques — direct ancestors of the brachial plexus and intercostal blocks taught in every regional-anesthesia rotation today. The self-experimentation exacted a brutal personal toll: Halsted developed a lifelong cocaine addiction that nearly derailed his career, and his attempt to treat it left him dependent on morphine for the rest of his life, a struggle he largely concealed while building Johns Hopkins's surgical program into a national model. It's a legacy worth sitting with alongside the technique itself — regional anesthesia's earliest history is inseparable from the people who tested it on their own bodies first, often at real personal cost.

🧠Board Question of the Week

A 19-year-old man develops masseter muscle rigidity, tachycardia to the 140s, and rapidly rising end-tidal CO2 shortly after induction with succinylcholine and sevoflurane for an emergency appendectomy. Which of the following is the most appropriate immediate pharmacologic intervention?

Correct answer: A — dantrolene is the only agent that reverses the underlying process in malignant hyperthermia

This presentation — masseter rigidity, tachycardia, and a rapidly rising end-tidal CO2 shortly after exposure to succinylcholine and a volatile agent — is classic for malignant hyperthermia (MH). Dantrolene works by binding the ryanodine receptor (RyR1) on the sarcoplasmic reticulum of skeletal muscle, blocking the pathological calcium release that drives the hypermetabolic crisis, and restoring normal excitation-contraction coupling. It's the only pharmacologic agent proven to reverse MH and should be dosed at 2.5 mg/kg IV, repeated as needed up to a cumulative 10 mg/kg, alongside stopping all triggering agents and initiating standard MH-crisis supportive care.

B is wrong because calcium chloride treats the cardiotoxicity of hyperkalemia — a downstream complication of MH, not the underlying mechanism — and giving calcium can actually worsen the intracellular calcium overload that's driving the crisis. C is wrong because continuing the case ignores the emergency: triggering agents must be stopped immediately and the case aborted, or converted to a non-triggering technique, once the patient is stabilized. D is wrong because physostigmine reverses anticholinergic (antimuscarinic) delirium, a different clinical picture entirely, and has no role in treating MH.
