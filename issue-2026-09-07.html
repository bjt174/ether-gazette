📰Headlines

The FTC's USAP Antitrust Settlement Is Still Just a Promise, Four Months Later

The federal government's most consequential antitrust case against private-equity-backed anesthesia consolidation remains unresolved. The FTC sued U.S. Anesthesia Partners and Welsh, Carson, Anderson & Stowe in 2023, alleging a decade-long roll-up scheme that bought up nearly every large anesthesia practice in Texas to create a single dominant provider capable of demanding higher prices — a case widely watched as a bellwether for PE-backed physician staffing consolidation nationally. On April 23, the FTC and USAP announced an "agreement in principle" to settle, and on May 26 the U.S. District Court for the Southern District of Texas stayed the case while the parties finalize terms. As of this writing, the settlement's substance remains confidential and no consent order has been entered — meaning the case that was supposed to "restore competition in Texas anesthesia markets" has sat in limbo for four months with nothing binding yet on the public record. A related patients' putative class action continues on a separate track. For SRNAs weighing employment with large multistate anesthesia groups, the case is worth tracking: whatever remedy eventually emerges will likely shape how aggressively PE-backed groups can consolidate regional anesthesia markets going forward.

Comments Close September 14 on CMS's Second Straight Anesthesia Pay Cut

The public comment window on CMS's Calendar Year 2027 Medicare Physician Fee Schedule proposed rule — which this newsletter flagged August 17 for floating anesthesia conversion factors of $20.4165 per unit for qualifying Advanced Alternative Payment Model participants and $20.2143 for everyone else, declines of roughly 0.9% and 1.4% from this year's rates — closes September 14. If finalized as proposed, it would mark a second consecutive year of anesthesia-specific downward pressure even as CMS's overall physician conversion factor updates hold flatter, a divergence ASA has been pressing the agency to justify. The rule also carries broader CY 2027 Physician Fee Schedule policy changes affecting how anesthesiologists and CRNAs participating in APMs get scored and paid. With the window closing next week, it's the last practical chance this cycle for individual clinicians and state societies to get objections on the record before CMS moves toward a final rule later this fall.

ASA's Fall Preview: Ketamine and Postpartum Depression, Ibuprofen and Delirium

Ahead of ANESTHESIOLOGY 2026 (October 16–20, San Diego), ASA released an early look at some of the roughly 1,000 scientific abstracts on the program. Two findings are drawing early attention: research suggesting perioperative ketamine given during cesarean delivery may ease early postpartum depressive symptoms, and separate findings that common over-the-counter analgesics like ibuprofen may lower the risk of postoperative delirium. Neither claim should be taken as settled — the broader published literature on perioperative ketamine and postpartum depression is genuinely mixed, with some meta-analyses finding a real signal and at least one systematic review finding no prophylactic effect on depression scores despite reduced opioid consumption, so this is a preview of abstracts, not a peer-reviewed consensus. The preview also flagged research on preoperative sleep aids reducing postoperative pain and opioid use, and on perioperative cannabis use potentially raising perioperative MI risk while lowering clot risk. Worth watching which of these hold up once the full abstracts and discussant commentary are available next month.

Nashville Welcomes Tennessee's First Anesthesiologist Assistant Trainees

Lipscomb University's College of Pharmacy began classes in August for the state's first-ever cohort of Certified Anesthesiologist Assistant students — 24 students selected from more than 300 applicants for the new two-year Master of Science in Anesthesiology program, led by inaugural program director Todd Christian. The program follows Tennessee's 2025 law licensing anesthesiologist assistants, effective January 1, 2026, which permits AAs to practice only under an anesthesiologist's direct supervision. Students will log more than 2,200 clinical hours and participate in at least 650 cases across cardiac, pediatric, and obstetric anesthesia before graduating. AAs remain a much smaller workforce than CRNAs nationally and are currently limited to practicing in roughly 20 states, all under the physician-led Anesthesia Care Team model — worth knowing as SRNAs evaluate how AA-training expansion in traditionally CRNA-friendly states like Tennessee might shape future staffing-model competition.

📚From the Literature

A National Study Finds Looser CRNA Scope-of-Practice Laws Raised Anesthesia Utilization — Broadly, Not Just in Underserved Areas

Using the COVID-19 pandemic's emergency scope-of-practice waivers as a natural experiment, researchers ran a difference-in-differences analysis comparing anesthesia-service utilization in areas that removed CRNA supervision or direction requirements against areas that kept them in place. Utilization rose 17% (about 18 more procedures per 1,000 members) in the areas that loosened supervision requirements, versus a 7% rise (about 9 more procedures per 1,000 members) where requirements stayed in place — a meaningful gap that supports the argument that removing supervision mandates measurably expands access. The more surprising finding: within states that loosened requirements, the utilization bump was similar in underserved and non-underserved areas alike, rather than concentrated in the access deserts that full-practice-authority advocacy typically emphasizes. That's a genuinely useful nuance for the scope-of-practice debate — it suggests looser CRNA SOPL boosts access broadly rather than functioning as a targeted rural-access fix on its own. One caveat worth flagging: the study leans on a pandemic-era natural experiment, so replication outside emergency conditions would strengthen the case before treating these effect sizes as durable.

Ghosh, P. P., Tarazi, W. W., Ume, N., Ferrara, E. E., Hogan, P., & Parker, E. D. (2026). Impact of scope of practice laws for certified registered nurse anesthetists on the utilization of anesthesia services. Health Services Research, 61(1), e70052. https://doi.org/10.1111/1475-6773.70052

⚖️Legislation & Scope Tracker

Florida's CRNA Autonomous-Practice Bill Clears the House, Then Dies Quietly in the Senate

HB 375 would have let qualified CRNAs register as autonomous advanced practice registered nurses and practice without physician-established protocols starting July 1, 2026 — a substantial break from Florida's current onsite-supervision framework. The bill actually cleared a full floor vote in the Florida House, 78–29, on February 4, a rarer feat than most state-level scope bills manage. But after being referred to the Senate Rules Committee on February 9, it never received a hearing and was recorded as "died in Rules" on March 13, ending its run for the 2026 session with no companion action expected before the legislature reconvenes. Florida joins Mississippi, Tennessee, and Virginia as supervision-required states with no successful scope expansion this cycle (our August 31 issue); the national count remains unchanged at 37 states plus DC opted out of the federal CMS supervision requirement, with 29 states plus DC granting full independent practice authority outright. A House floor win that stalls in Senate Rules without a hearing is its own kind of signal — the votes for full practice authority may exist in the chamber, but the leadership will to schedule it evidently doesn't yet.

💡Knowledge Pearl

Ketamine doesn't put the brain to sleep the way propofol does — it disconnects parts of it from each other. Unlike GABAergic agents, which produce global cortical depression, ketamine is a noncompetitive antagonist at the NMDA glutamate receptor. Blocking NMDA receptors functionally disconnects the thalamocortical pathways from the limbic system, producing what's classically termed "dissociative anesthesia": a trance-like, cataleptic state in which the patient appears disconnected from their surroundings rather than unconscious in the propofol sense. Because ketamine doesn't suppress the brainstem respiratory centers or the airway-protective reflexes the way GABAergic agents do, spontaneous ventilation and airway tone are typically preserved even at induction doses — part of why it remains a go-to agent for austere or prehospital settings, pediatric procedural sedation, and patients you don't want to fully paralyze and intubate.

Board-relevant pearl: know why ketamine's hemodynamic profile isn't as reliable as its reputation suggests. Ketamine indirectly raises heart rate, blood pressure, and cardiac output by inhibiting neuronal reuptake of norepinephrine — an indirect sympathomimetic effect, not direct receptor agonism. That effect depends on the patient having intact catecholamine stores to release. In a patient who has been in prolonged shock, sepsis, or another catecholamine-depleted state, those stores can be exhausted, unmasking ketamine's own intrinsic direct myocardial depressant effect — meaning the same drug that reliably raises blood pressure in a healthy trauma patient can drop it in a chronically critically ill one. This is exactly the kind of exception that shows up on exams precisely because it contradicts the "ketamine is always hemodynamically stable" teaching point.

🕰️This Week in Anesthesia History
September 10, 1862

On September 10, 1862, James Tayloe Gwathmey was born in Roanoke, Virginia — an unlikely start for a man later called the "Father of Modern Anesthesia." Before medicine, Gwathmey was expelled from the Virginia Military Institute, joined an acrobatic troupe, and taught gymnastics at YMCAs in Iowa and Nebraska. He didn't earn his M.D. from Vanderbilt until 1899, at nearly 37. Moving to New York City in 1902, he made a deliberate and at-the-time unusual choice: he built one of the first full-time private anesthesia practices in the country, establishing clinical independence from the surgeons he worked alongside — a professional stance that still echoes in how anesthesia providers, physician and CRNA alike, think about their relationship to surgical teams today. In 1914 he co-authored Anesthesia with chemist Charles Baskerville, the field's first comprehensive textbook, and became the first president of the American Association of Anesthetists, founded in 1912. He also refined nitrous oxide-oxygen delivery apparatus, cutting its weight from 50 pounds to 14.5, and advocated for tailoring combined anesthetic techniques to the individual patient rather than a one-size-fits-all approach — a principle that reads as strikingly modern more than a century later.

🧠Board Question of the Week

A 45-year-old man arrives for emergent exploratory laparotomy after two hours of ongoing hemorrhage and resuscitation following a motor vehicle collision; his blood pressure remains 78/44 mmHg despite fluids and blood products. Which of the following best describes the expected hemodynamic effect if ketamine is used for induction in this patient?

Correct answer: C — ketamine's hemodynamic effect depends on catecholamine stores that prolonged shock can deplete

Ketamine's classic hypertensive, sympathomimetic profile is indirect: it inhibits neuronal reuptake of norepinephrine, raising circulating catecholamine levels rather than acting as a direct adrenergic agonist. That mechanism requires the patient to have endogenous catecholamine stores available to release. In a patient who has been in shock for an extended period, those stores can already be significantly depleted, unmasking ketamine's own intrinsic direct negative inotropic effect on the myocardium — producing hypotension instead of the expected pressor response. This is a well-recognized exception to the "ketamine is hemodynamically stable" teaching point and a common way exam writers test whether a candidate understands the mechanism rather than just the reputation.

D is wrong because ketamine's hemodynamic risk in this scenario is not vagally mediated bradycardia; the concern is depleted sympathetic reserve unmasking direct depression.
